Contributing factors — two to five systemic causes (a process gap, a tooling limitation, a missing test, a misrouted alert), each framed as a property of the system, not a person.     { q: "Why does the unit prefer 'contributing factors' over 'the root cause'?", a: "Because 'root cause' is grammatically singular, and that singularity is the trap — it nudges everyone to stop at the first plausible factor and pin it on whoever was nearest the keyboard. Complex systems fail multi-causally: the deploy, the flaky test that let the CI gate skip, the config flag with no staging coverage, and the misrouted alert all had to line up; no single one caused it, their interaction did. Dekker calls 'error as the cause of failure' the old view; the new view treats error as a symptom of deeper systemic conditions. Switching to 'contributing factors' is not pedantry — it changes where the room looks and which fixes get written." },
    { q: "Summarize Allspaw's 'Infinite Hows' critique of five-whys.", a: "Five-whys asks 'why' repeatedly to reach a single root cause, and for complex systems that is actively misleading. Drawing on Dekker and Leveson, Allspaw argues that each 'why' demands a justification, and justifications point at people, so the chain drags you toward a single linear story, hindsight bias, and ultimately blame. His reframe is to ask 'how' instead — the infinite hows. 'How' questions surface the conditions that made an action reasonable at the time: the hidden expert knowledge people used, the efficiency-versus-thoroughness tradeoffs they navigated under pressure. The shift from 'why did you do that?' to 'how did doing that make sense given what you knew?' is the difference between an interrogation and an investigation — and you can fix a system, not a person." },
    { q: "What distinguishes a real action item from a non-item, and why does it matter for the cost of the retro?", a: "A real action item is specific, has a single named owner, carries a due date, and has a verifiable definition of done, so it can be tracked to closure. Non-items like 'be more careful' or 'the team should improve' have no owner, no date, and nothing to verify; they pad the list and never ship. It matters because the document is the cheap part and the follow-through is the expensive, valuable part. Reported norms put action-item completion below 50%, often under 40% within 90 days, with repeat-incident rates around 35–50%. If items are filed but never shipped, you pay the full cost of the retro and buy nothing — the same outage recurs and engineers learn that postmortems are theater." },

Publish fast while memory is fresh (Google's SRE workbook suggests under 48 hours). Then treat action items like any other prioritized work: a single owner, a due date, and 30/60/90-day follow-ups, targeting completion well above 85%.
