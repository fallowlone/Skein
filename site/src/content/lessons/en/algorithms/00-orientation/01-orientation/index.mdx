## A recipe a machine could follow

An algorithm is a **recipe**: a clear sequence of steps that takes some **input** and produces an **output**. The key word is *unambiguous* — every step must be so clear that there is no guessing about what to do next. A cooking recipe says "bake for 20 minutes," not "bake for a while." That precision is what lets a computer — which cannot guess — follow it exactly.

Three things make a list of steps a real algorithm: it must **finish** (not run forever), each step must be **clear**, and it must produce the **right output** for any valid input.

- **Input** — what you start with (a list of names, two numbers, a word to find).
- **Steps** — the precise, ordered actions, with no "figure it out" gaps.
- **Output** — the result the steps produce (the sorted list, the sum, the page number).
- **Finite** — it must stop after a limited number of steps.

## Why the same task has fast and slow recipes

Here is the part that makes algorithms worth studying: one task can have many correct recipes, and they are not equally good. Finding a word by reading every page works — but for a 1000-page dictionary it could take 1000 checks. The "flip to the middle and halve" method gets the same answer in about 10 checks. Both are correct. One is a hundred times faster.

That gap is **efficiency**, and it explodes as the input grows. A slow recipe might be fine for 10 items and hopeless for 10 million. Learning algorithms is learning to see that gap — to ask not just "does this work?" but "how does the cost grow as the problem gets bigger?" That single question is what the rest of this track trains.
