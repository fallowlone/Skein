USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) describes each resource from the kernel's side — it tells you a resource is stressed, but not whether the user notices (a CPU at 95% on a batch worker is fine; on the request path it is an incident).     { q: "What does Saturation measure, and why is it more diagnostic than Utilization?", a: "Utilization is the average fraction of time a resource was busy; saturation is the amount of queued work it cannot service yet — run-queue length, I/O queue depth, or PSI stall time. Saturation is more diagnostic because waiting work is the leading indicator of latency: every job in the queue pays the wait, so a disk at 80% utilization with queue depth 50 is far worse than one at 95% with queue depth 1. A resource can be 100% utilized and healthy if nothing is waiting (it is just keeping pace), but a growing queue means users are already paying." },
    { q: "What is PSI, what do 'some' and 'full' mean, and why did it replace load average for saturation?", a: "Pressure Stall Information (Linux 4.20+, exposed at /proc/pressure/{cpu,memory,io}) reports the percentage of wall-clock time tasks were stalled waiting for a resource, averaged over 10/60/300 s. 'some' means at least one task was stalled; 'full' means all non-idle tasks were stalled — full is the severe signal, and memory or I/O full sustained above zero is a crunch even when free-RAM looks plentiful (the kernel is thrashing reclaim). PSI replaced load average because load average mixes runnable and uninterruptible tasks, scales oddly with core count, and is a slow EMA — PSI normalises by wall-clock time and is core-count independent, which is why Kubernetes uses it for eviction." },
    { q: "What does the golden-signals' Saturation add that RED's three letters miss, with a concrete example?", a: "Google's four golden signals are Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation — the first three map onto RED's Duration, Rate, Errors, and Saturation is the addition: how full the service is relative to a logical capacity limit. Unlike USE's physical-resource saturation, this is service-level: connection-pool occupancy, in-flight request count, queue depth. A service can sit at 50% CPU with its connection pool at 100% — users queue, RED Duration may still look fine momentarily, and USE on the host shows nothing. For async/queue systems the key extra signal is consumer lag / queue-depth-in-seconds, which captures the wait before processing that RED's per-job Duration never sees." },
    { q: "What is cardinality, why is one unbounded label catastrophic, and which labels are safe?", a: "Cardinality is the number of unique label-value combinations on a metric; each combination is a separate time series stored at roughly 3 KB of RAM in the TSDB head and billed per active series in hosted backends. Series count is the product of all label cardinalities, so a single unbounded label (user_id, request_id, error_message content) multiplies an existing 200-series metric into millions, crashing the TSDB or adding tens of thousands of dollars overnight. Safe labels are bounded and actionable: route template, HTTP method, status_class, service, region. High-cardinality data lives in logs and traces instead — and PII in labels is a data leak on top of a cost problem." },

When histogram_quantile shows p99 at 800 ms, clicking the spike in Grafana surfaces the exemplar — a trace_id from a request that landed in that bucket — and one click jumps to the full span tree. Cardinality stays flat because the trace_id rides along with the observation rather than multiplying series. This is the designed bridge between aggregated metrics (100% coverage) and sampled traces." },
