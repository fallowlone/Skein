An SLO is the target for that SLI, a percentage the team commits to (e.g. 99.9%). The error budget is what remains below the target: budget = (1 − SLO) × total_events. OR (Approach 4) fires when either is high, combining the noise of the short window with the slow reset of the long one. MWMBR uses AND: the long window confirms the burn is real and sustained (noise resistance), and the short window confirms it is still ongoing right now (fast reset, clears within ~5 minutes of fix). AND is the mechanism that bounds both detection latency and recovery latency." },

With correlated failures (shared database, regional event) the real ceiling is lower still, which is why a journey-level SLI at the API gateway is the authoritative measurement. Levers: (1) Idempotent retries with backoff — one retry turns a 0.1% per-attempt failure into roughly (0.001)^2 ≈ 0.0001%, but requires an idempotency key so a retried charge does not duplicate; cost is added latency. (2) Parallel hedging — fire two replicas, take the first success; both failing is (0.001)^2 = 99.9999%, but costs 2x traffic and you must cancel or dedupe the extra response. Signatures matter because without a director-level signer the policy is a suggestion that on-call gets pressured to override during release crunches; with the signature, 'the policy says no' ends the argument. The SLA is different: it is a contractual commitment to customers with financial penalties, and it must be looser than the internal SLO by 0.05-0.5pp so the team has buffer to detect and fix before a contractual breach. Four remedies: (1) Synthetic probes (preferred) — a probe every 10s gives ~360 stable requests/hour so the denominator never collapses, though probes cover availability, not full journey correctness. (4) Hybrid event-based alerting — keep the SLO for reporting but alert on raw counts ('more than 5 failures in 30 minutes') for very low-traffic surfaces.
