    { q: "What does an exponent count, and why is reading 2³ as 6 wrong?", a: "An exponent counts how many copies of the base are multiplied together — it is not itself a factor. So 2³ means three 2s multiplied: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Reading it as 6 treats the exponent as something to multiply the base by (2 × 3), which is the single most common slip. The correct question is always: how many copies of the base, then multiply that many of them. Each step up the exponent multiplies by the base once more, which is why 2¹ = 2, 2² = 4, 2³ = 8 — doubling each time, not adding." },
    { q: "Why does any (nonzero) base raised to the power 0 equal 1?", a: "Follow the step-down pattern. For base 2: 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2 — each step down divides by the base. To keep that rule unbroken, the next step down must be 2 ÷ 2 = 1, so 2⁰ = 1. The same staircase works for any base: 9¹ = 9, then 9 ÷ 9 = 1, so 9⁰ = 1. Defining it as 1 (not 0) is the only choice that keeps 'step down, divide by the base' consistent all the way down. It is a definition forced by the pattern, not an arbitrary rule." },
    { q: "Why does the exponent of a power of ten equal its number of zeros?", a: "Each time you raise the power of ten by one, you multiply by 10 once more, and multiplying a whole number by 10 shifts every digit one place left, dropping a fresh 0 into the ones place. So 10² = 100 (two zeros), 10³ = 1000 (three zeros), 10⁶ = 1000000 (six zeros). The exponent and the zero count rise in lockstep because both count the same thing: how many times you multiplied by ten. To write 10ⁿ, put a 1 and then n zeros." },
    { q: "What is scientific form, and how do you write 50000 in it?", a: "Scientific form writes a large round number as a small leading number times a power of ten, which keeps big numbers short and easy to size up — you read the power of ten first to see how large, then the small number for the detail. For 50000: count the zeros — there are four — so the power of ten is 10⁴ (which is 10000), and the leading digit is 5. So 50000 = 5 × 10⁴. Check: 5 × 10000 = 50000. The 5 carries the leading digit; the 10⁴ carries the size." },
    { q: "What does a square root ask, and how does it relate to squaring?", a: "Squaring is the forward question — start with a number and multiply it by itself: 3² = 3 × 3 = 9. The square root is that exact question read backwards: what number, multiplied by itself, gives 9? The answer is 3, written √9 = 3. So the square root undoes squaring the way subtraction undoes addition; 3² = 9 and √9 = 3 are one fact in two directions. It is not halving: √16 asks which number times itself is 16 (that is 4), not 16 ÷ 2 = 8." },
    { q: "Which numbers are perfect squares, and how do you estimate the root of a number that is not one?", a: "Perfect squares are the numbers whose square root is a whole number: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, and so on — each is some whole number times itself. Most numbers are not perfect squares, so their roots fall between two whole numbers. To estimate such a root, trap it between the two nearest perfect squares. For √50: 7² = 49 (just under) and 8² = 64 (above), so √50 lies between 7 and 8, and since 50 is just above 49 it is just a little more than 7." },
