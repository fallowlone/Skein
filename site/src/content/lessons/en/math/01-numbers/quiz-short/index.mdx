The numbers you count with — 1, 2, 3, 4, ... — are the natural numbers, and they never run out: there is always a next one." },

The number line decides which: the number that sits further to the right is the bigger one, because numbers grow to the right. So to compare 4 and 9 you find both on the line; 9 is further right, so 9 is greater and 4 is less. The two facts '9 is greater than 4' and '4 is less than 9' say the same thing from each number's point of view." },
    { q: "How do the greater-than and less-than signs work, and what is the trick for never flipping them?", a: "Greater than is written with the sign whose open side faces left, less than with the open side facing right, and equal with two equal bars. The trick: think of the sign as an open mouth, and the wide-open side always faces the bigger number while the narrow point touches the smaller one. So 7 greater-than 4 has the open side toward 7, and 4 less-than 7 has the point toward 4. Checking every comparison this way means you can never write the sign the wrong way round." },
    { q: "Explain place value: why is the digit 4 worth 40 in one number and only 4 in another?", a: "A number is built from ten digits (0 through 9), and a digit's worth depends on which place it sits in. Reading from the right, the places are ones, tens, hundreds — each worth ten times the one to its right. A digit's place value is the digit times its place. So the 4 in 40 sits in the tens place and is worth 4 tens = 40, while the 4 in 4 sits in the ones place and is worth 4. The whole number is the sum of its place values: 247 is 200 + 40 + 7." },
    { q: "What job does the digit 0 do in a number like 305, and what changes if you remove it?", a: "Zero is a placeholder: it holds a place empty so the other digits stay where they belong. In 305 the 0 holds the tens place empty, which forces the 3 into the hundreds place (worth 300) and keeps the 5 in the ones place. If you remove the 0 you get 35: the 3 slides back to the tens place and is now worth only 30. So the 0 is not 'nothing' — it is exactly what makes three hundred and five look different from thirty-five." },
    { q: "What is a number line, and why must its gaps be equal?", a: "A number line is a straight line with a start mark at 0 and an evenly spaced mark for every number after it, growing to the right. Each whole number has exactly one spot, and no two numbers share a spot. The gaps must be equal because one step is meant to be one count: moving one mark to the right is counting up one, one mark left is counting down one. If the gaps were uneven the picture would lie about distance — a number could look close to one neighbour and far from another even though both are one step away. Equal gaps are what make the line honest." },
