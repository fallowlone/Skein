## Why not just call the other part directly?

When part A calls part B directly, A is stuck until B answers. If B is slow, A is slow. If B is down, A fails too. Their fates are tied together — that is called being **coupled**. A queue cuts the tie. A writes a message into the queue and moves on; B reads it whenever it is ready. A does not know or care if B is fast, slow, or briefly offline. If B is down for a minute, the messages just wait in line. This is **decoupling**: each side can be slow, restart, or scale up without breaking the other.

## Producer, queue, consumer

A queue has three parts, and the names are worth memorizing because the whole track uses them:

- A **producer** is the part that puts work in — it writes a message and is done.
- The **queue** is the line in the middle that holds messages until someone takes them.
- A **consumer** is the part that takes work out — it reads a message and processes it.

The producer and consumer never talk to each other directly. They only ever talk to the queue. That single rule is what buys you the decoupling.

## "Do it later" is the whole point

Because the producer returns the instant it drops a message, the work happens **asynchronously** — later, not right now, on someone else's clock. That gives you three things almost for free. The user gets an instant response. A traffic spike piles up harmlessly in the queue instead of crushing the consumer. And if the consumer crashes, the messages are still waiting when it comes back. You will spend this track learning the hard parts: making sure a message is processed exactly the right number of times, and what to do when one keeps failing.

<Recap lang="en">
When one part of a system calls another directly, their fates are tied: a slow or crashed callee drags the caller down with it. A queue breaks that tie. A producer drops a message into a line and returns instantly; a consumer reads it later, on its own schedule. The two sides only ever talk to the queue, never to each other — that is decoupling, and it buys instant responses, safe handling of traffic spikes, and survival across crashes. The rest of this track is about doing it correctly: delivery guarantees, ordering, and handling failures.
</Recap>
