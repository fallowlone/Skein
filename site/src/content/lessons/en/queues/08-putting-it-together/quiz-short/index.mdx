    { q: "Why is idempotency, not exactly-once delivery, the invariant that holds the whole pipeline together?", a: "Because every honest hop on the wire is at-least-once and there is no global exactly-once knob to turn. The CDC relay resumes from its last confirmed LSN, so on restart it can re-emit a row it published just before crashing. The consumer commits its Kafka offset only after the handler succeeds, so a crash between processing and commit replays the same event to the next consumer after a rebalance. Chasing exactly-once delivery means fighting the Two Generals' impossibility. Instead you accept duplicates as the contract and neutralise them at the consumer: record a dedup key (the event id or a business idempotency key) under a unique constraint inside the same transaction as the side effect, so a redelivery hits the constraint and becomes a no-op. For external effects you propagate the key downstream — Stripe's Idempotency-Key returns the original charge for 24 hours instead of creating a second. CDC tails the WAL via a logical replication slot, delivering changes within milliseconds of commit with zero query load on the table. The slot is dangerous because its promise — Postgres will not recycle WAL past the slot's confirmed LSN — is unconditional and holds even when the consumer is dead. If the connector crashes, OOMs, or lags (or a long-running transaction freezes restart_lsn while the connector looks healthy), WAL stops being recycled and piles up. On a busy OLTP box doing 20–50 GB of WAL per hour, a healthy disk fills in under two hours, and a full disk makes Postgres reject all writes — a full primary outage.     { q: "Where does per-key ordering live in the pipeline, and name three ways it silently breaks in production.", a: "Ordering lives at the partition: Kafka guarantees order only within a partition, chosen by hash(key) % N, so keying the topic by order_id puts an order's whole history on one partition processed by one consumer — a cancel can never overtake its create for that order. First, raising the partition count rehashes most keys, so a key's new records route to a different partition than its history and the two streams are no longer ordered — repartition by migrating to a new topic, never resize in place. Second, retries and DLQ replays reinsert a message out of band: message 5 fails, 6/7/8 commit, then 5 is replayed and lands after its successors — so you replay rate-limited and lean on a version guard. Third, a non-idempotent producer with max.in.flight greater than 1 lets a retried batch land after a later one that succeeded — which is exactly why KIP-185 made the idempotent producer the default. Uber's reliable-reprocessing design uses tiered retry topics feeding a DLQ for exactly this: a poison message is quarantined out of the live stream after its budget runs out, so the rest of the partition keeps flowing. <Recap lang="en">
If you reconstructed each answer from memory, you hold the track's spine: the outbox makes the write and its event atomic in one local transaction; CDC ships those rows at-least-once and its replication slot is the loaded gun you must alert on and cap; the topic preserves per-key order by keying on order_id, and that order breaks when you repartition, when retries and DLQ replays reinsert out of band, or when a non-idempotent producer reorders; the consumer group delivers at-least-once by committing after processing, so handlers must be idempotent; transient failures retry against a budget while poison messages go to the DLQ; and the UI tells the truth about the consistency window. Idempotency is the thread, and observability lives at the seams — a correlation id end to end, lag watched by trend, and alarms on DLQ depth and slot lag. Now when you walk into a design review for any event-driven system, you know the six questions to ask — and the one invariant that, if missing, makes all six answers irrelevant.
</Recap>
