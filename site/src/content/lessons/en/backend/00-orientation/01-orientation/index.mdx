## Request in, response out

Two computers are talking. The one in front of you — the browser or phone app — is the **frontend**. It sends a **request**: a short message that says what it wants, like "give me my messages" or "save this note." It travels over the network to another computer that is always on, called the **server**. The program running on that server is the **backend**. It reads the request, does the work, and sends back a **response**: the data, or just a confirmation.

That is the whole loop. Every button you press in any app is, underneath, one request going out and one response coming back.

- **Request** — the question the frontend asks ("log me in", "list my orders").
- **Server** — the always-on computer the backend lives on.
- **Backend** — the program that decides what the answer is.
- **Response** — the answer sent back, plus a status like "ok" or "not allowed".

## Why it cannot just be on your phone

If the program lived only on your phone, your friend's phone could never see your message, and closing the app would lose everything. The backend exists because some work has to happen in one shared, trusted place: it holds the real data, checks who is allowed to do what, and answers many people at the same time without mixing them up. The frontend is a window; the backend is the source of truth behind the glass.

Doing this well is harder than it sounds — thousands of requests can arrive at once, the network can drop, and the server can crash mid-task. The rest of this track is about how a backend stays correct and fast under all of that.
