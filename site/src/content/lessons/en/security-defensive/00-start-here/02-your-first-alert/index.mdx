## Prevention says no, detection takes notes

A firewall rule, a locked door, input validation: these are prevention. They look at an action and refuse it. Prevention is binary and silent — it either stopped the thing or it never saw it.

Detection starts where prevention ends. It assumes something WILL get past the blocks — a stolen password beats every login form ever built — and so it records what happens and looks for shapes in the record:

Neither works alone. Prevention without detection is a wall nobody patrols: the first attacker who climbs it operates invisibly. Detection without prevention is an alarm on an open door: you watch everything walk in. Defense is always both — which is the subject of the next lesson.

## The three-part shape of every alert

Every working alert, from a login page to a national CERT, has the same three parts. Learn them once here; unit `01-logging-monitoring-detection` spends a whole unit on part one.

**1. A record.** Something writes down what happened: a log line per login attempt with time, address, and result. No record, no detection — you cannot alert on what you never wrote down.

**2. A rule.** A precise condition over the record: "more than 20 failed logins from one address in one minute." Vague rules ("suspicious activity") alert on everything and mean nothing; the rule is where noise becomes signal.

**3. A response.** Someone or something acts: block the address for an hour, page the on-call, open an incident ticket. An alert nobody responds to is just a more expensive log line — the response closes the blue-team loop of detect, triage, respond.

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
Why "more than 20 in a minute" and not "any failure at all"? Because real users mistype passwords — alerting on every failure would page the team dozens of times a day, and humans rapidly learn to ignore alarms that cry wolf. This has a name: alert fatigue. It kills more defenses than any attacker technique: the signal was there, the rule fired, and nobody looked because the last fifty fires were nothing. Good detection is calibrated — sensitive enough to catch the attack, quiet enough that a firing alert still means "look now."
</Inset>
