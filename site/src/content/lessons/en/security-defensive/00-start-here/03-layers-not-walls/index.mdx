<Hook>
A bank does not protect its vault with one giant lock. It has the lock, the alarm that screams when the lock is touched, the cameras recording who touched it, the dye pack that ruins the money if it leaves, and the insurance that pays out if everything else fails. <Crux>Defense in depth layers independent controls — preventive, detective, corrective — so that beating one control only walks the attacker into the next one, louder.</Crux>

## One house, three jobs

Take a house at night. The locked door is preventive: it says no. The motion-sensor light and the camera are detective: they notice and record. The insurance policy and the spare key with a neighbor are corrective: they limit the damage and restore the state after the worst happens.

The point is not the count. The point is independence: the camera runs on a different circuit than the door lock, so cutting power to defeat one does not defeat the other. In security terms, controls must not share a failure mode — two checks that both trust the same password file fall together, which is one layer wearing a costume, not two.

## Assume breach: design from the inside

Defense in depth starts from assume breach: act as if the attacker is already past one layer, and ask what stops them at the next. A stolen password beats prevention (the login form accepted it) — but detection sees the 3 a.m. login from a new country, and correction (session revoke, forced reset) bounds what the stolen session can do.

This flips the beginner's question from "how do I keep them out?" to "when they get in — and the plan assumes when, not if — which layer catches it, and what does recovery cost?" Unit `03-hardening-defense-in-depth` turns this mindset into concrete defaults; every layer there is justified by the failure of the one before it.

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
Why not spend the whole budget on one perfect wall? Because perfect walls don't exist, and a single wall concentrates all your risk in one mechanism: one bug, one stolen key, one misconfiguration, and there is nothing behind it. Layers diversify risk the way investments diversify money — each layer can be mediocre at its job as long as defeating all of them silently costs more than the attacker gains. Security spending is not about the strongest control; it is about the cheapest combination the attacker cannot afford to beat quietly.
</Inset>

<Recap lang="en">
Defense in depth means independent layers with different jobs: preventive controls refuse, detective controls notice and record, corrective controls bound damage and restore. Independence is the whole game — shared failure modes are one layer in costume. Design from assume breach: the attacker already beat one layer, so name what catches them next and what recovery costs. Unit 03 turns this mindset into hardened defaults; it will assume you can already tell a layer from a wall.
</Recap>
