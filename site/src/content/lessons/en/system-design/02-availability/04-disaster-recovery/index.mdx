## Blast radius: what HA does not cover

The previous lessons built **high availability**: redundancy and failover so a dead process, host, or zone is absorbed in seconds. HA assumes the failure is *local* and the *data is correct*. Disaster recovery (DR) covers the cases where one of those assumptions breaks:

- **Site loss.** A whole region goes down: power, network, a control-plane bug at the provider. Every zone you replicated across is inside the blast radius.
- **Data loss by your own hand.** A bad migration, a buggy deploy writing garbage, an operator dropping a table. Replication faithfully copies the damage everywhere.
- **Adversarial loss.** Ransomware or a compromised credential that deletes resources, including the backups if they live in the same account.

The rule that falls out: **replication is not a backup.** Replication protects against losing a *copy*. A backup protects against losing the *truth*, so it must be point-in-time (you can go back to before the damage), isolated (a separate account or region the attacker and the bad script can't reach), and immutable for its retention window.

## The two numbers: RPO and RTO

Every DR conversation starts with two business numbers, not technology:

- **RPO (recovery point objective):** how much data, measured in time, you can afford to lose. RPO of 1 hour means after a disaster you may come back with state as of up to an hour ago. It is set by how often data leaves the blast radius: snapshot frequency, log shipping interval, or replication lag.
- **RTO (recovery time objective):** how long you can be down before the business is hurt unacceptably. It is set by how much of the recovery site already exists and how much has to be built, copied, warmed, and switched over during the incident.

They are independent. A bank's ledger may need RPO ≈ 0 (no lost transactions) but can tolerate RTO of an hour. An ad-serving system may tolerate losing an hour of click logs but needs RTO of minutes. Pinning both numbers *per service* is the first deliverable, because the cost curve is steep: each step closer to zero roughly multiplies the bill.

## Four strategies, one cost ladder

The standard taxonomy (from AWS's DR whitepaper, but it applies to any provider) is a ladder where you pay continuously for idle capacity to buy a shorter RTO:

1. **Backup & restore.** Data is backed up to another region; nothing else runs there. On disaster you provision infrastructure from code, restore data, and switch traffic. RTO: hours. RPO: the backup interval. Cheapest, and only viable if infrastructure-as-code actually recreates everything.
2. **Pilot light.** The data tier is continuously replicated into the recovery region and kept live, but the compute tier is off or scaled to zero. On disaster you scale up the app servers and switch over. RTO: tens of minutes. RPO: replication lag (seconds to minutes).
3. **Warm standby.** A complete, scaled-down copy of production runs in the recovery region and serves at least health checks. On disaster you scale it out and shift traffic. RTO: minutes. RPO: replication lag.
4. **Multi-site active-active.** Both regions serve real traffic all the time. Losing one means the others absorb its load. RTO: near zero (it's a failover, not a recovery). RPO: zero for data that is synchronously replicated, replication lag otherwise. Most expensive, and it forces you to solve multi-region writes.

## RPO is replication lag, and sync across regions is expensive

Within a region, synchronous replication across zones costs a millisecond or two per write. Across regions it costs a full round trip, often 60–150 ms between continents, on *every* committed write. So most multi-region systems replicate **asynchronously**, and their real RPO is whatever the lag was at the moment the region died. If lag spikes to 40 seconds during a traffic surge and the region fails then, you lose 40 seconds of writes, regardless of what the design document says. Monitor replication lag as an SLO, because it *is* your RPO.

Systems that genuinely need RPO = 0 across regions (Spanner, CockroachDB, a quorum spread over three regions) pay the cross-region round trip on writes and accept it as the price. This is PACELC from the data-distribution unit in practice: even without a partition, you choose between latency and consistency.

## Active-active forces the write question

Read-only traffic is easy to serve from two regions. Writes are not: if a user can write the same row in both regions at once, you have the split-brain problem from the failover lesson, permanently, by design. Three standard answers:

- **Home region per entity.** Each user, tenant, or account is pinned to one region that owns its writes. Other regions serve reads and forward writes. On regional failure, ownership is reassigned. Simple, and it's what most large SaaS systems do.
- **Conflict-free data types or last-writer-wins.** Allow writes everywhere and merge. Works for counters, sets, and presence; dangerous for money.
- **Global consensus.** A single logical database with a quorum spanning regions. Correct and simple to reason about, slow on writes.

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
Why do regional failovers so often fail even when a standby exists? Because the recovery plan depends on the thing that just broke. If shifting traffic requires calling the cloud provider's control-plane API in the failing region, or scaling up requires launching instances through a service that is degraded region-wide, the runbook stalls at step one. AWS calls the fix **static stability**: the recovery site should already have the capacity it needs and should not need to make *any* control-plane changes to start serving. Pre-provisioned capacity, data-plane-only failover (health-checked DNS records that flip on their own), and credentials and runbooks stored outside the failing region. Design the recovery path to work when everything you'd normally use to operate is unavailable.
</Inset>

## Untested recovery is not recovery

The Google SRE book's data-integrity chapter says it bluntly: nobody really wants backups, they want *restores*. Backups that have never been restored are a hypothesis. The practice:

- **Restore drills on a schedule.** Restore a real backup into an isolated environment, run integrity checks, and *measure the actual RTO*. The number you measure is your RTO; the number in the design doc is a wish.
- **Game days and region evacuations.** Shift production traffic out of a region on purpose, during business hours, with everyone watching. Netflix and others do this routinely; it's the only way to discover the hard-coded endpoint and the missing IAM permission before a real disaster does.
- **The 3-2-1 rule.** Three copies, two different media or services, one off-site (a different account and region). Add immutability (object lock, write-once retention) so a compromised credential can't delete the backups.

<Recap lang="en">
**High availability** absorbs local failures and assumes the data is right; **disaster recovery** covers losing a whole region, damaging your own data with a bad deploy, or having resources deleted by an attacker. That is why **replication is not a backup**: it copies corruption and deletes as faithfully as good writes, so you also need **point-in-time, isolated, immutable backups**. Every DR design starts from two business numbers per service: **RPO**, the data you can afford to lose (set by backup frequency or replication lag), and **RTO**, the time you can be down (set by how much of the recovery site already runs). The **strategy ladder** trades continuous cost for shorter RTO: backup and restore (hours), pilot light (tens of minutes), warm standby (minutes), multi-site active-active (near zero). Cross-region sync replication costs a full round trip per write, so most systems replicate async and their **real RPO is the lag** at the moment of failure. Active-active forces you to answer multi-region writes with a home region per entity, mergeable data types, or global consensus. Recovery must be **statically stable**, with no dependence on the failing region's control plane, and it must be **rehearsed**: restore drills that measure the real RTO and game days that evacuate a region on purpose. The hook's team had excellent HA and no DR: a replicated `DELETE`, an unrestored same-account snapshot, and a runbook nobody had ever run.
</Recap>
