<Hook>
You've tuned your Kafka producer's linger.ms from 0 to 10ms and throughput jumped 10x. But why does adding a tiny delay boost throughput so much? The answer lies in the two-dimensional window that controls batching.
</Hook>

<Crux>Batch size and max wait time — whichever hits first sends the batch. Larger windows buy throughput at the cost of tail-latency.</Crux>

<Explanation>
Every batching system has two knobs: max-size (bytes or records) and max-wait (time). Whichever triggers first — the batch fills up or the timer expires — sends the batch.

Without max-size, a slow producer holds the batch until the timer expires, wasting throughput. Without max-wait, a fast producer builds huge batches that may exceed downstream capacity.

Math: Let fixed cost F (per-call overhead) and variable cost V*n (payload size). Time for N separate operations: N*(F + V*n). Time for one batched N: F + V*(N*n). Speedup: (N*F + N*V*n) / (F + V*N*n). When F dominates (small payloads), speedup → N. When V dominates (large payloads), speedup → 1. Break-even ≈ F = V*n. For ~1KB packets and ~50µs RTT, a 100-item batch reduces wall time from ~5ms to ~150µs — a 33x gain.

Defaults: 10-100ms wait, 64KB-1MB size, tuned by replaying production traffic in staging.
</Explanation>

<KeyTakeaway>Batching windows use max-size and max-wait; whichever triggers first sends the batch. Tune to hit SLO latency ceiling, not maximum throughput.</KeyTakeaway>

<Recap lang="en">
The two-dimensional window (max-size + max-wait) is the core of batching systems. It adapts to varying load: at high load, size triggers first; at low load, time triggers first. The break-even point depends on the ratio of fixed to variable cost. Proper tuning requires measuring your operation's fixed and variable costs, then adjusting the window to meet latency SLOs while maximizing throughput.
</Recap>
