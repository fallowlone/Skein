## More than one computer, one job

A **distributed system** is a group of separate computers that work together so they look, to a user, like a single service. Each computer is called a **node**. The nodes do not share memory; the only way they coordinate is by sending **messages** to each other over a network.

Why bother with the extra machines? Three reasons keep coming back:

- **Scale** — one computer can only do so much work; many can do more.
- **Availability** — if one node dies, the others keep serving.
- **Closeness** — putting nodes near users makes the service faster for them.

The catch is that you trade a single, simple computer for a crowd that has to agree.

## Why agreeing is hard

On one computer, "what is true right now" is obvious — there is one memory, one clock, one source of truth. Across many computers that breaks, for reasons that never fully go away:

- **Machines fail independently.** One node can crash or restart while the others keep running. The survivors cannot tell a crashed node apart from a slow one.
- **The network is not reliable.** Messages get delayed, lost, reordered, or duplicated. Assuming "the network always works" is a classic, costly mistake.
- **There is no shared clock.** Each node has its own slightly-wrong clock, so "which event happened first?" has no easy answer.

Put together: nodes must reach **agreement** — on who is in charge, on which write wins, on whether an order really happened — using only late, lossy messages, while any of them might vanish at any moment. That is the whole subject.

## The mental model to carry up

Hold one picture for the rest of this track: **a room of people who can only pass written notes, where notes sometimes go missing, arrive out of order, or come twice — and any person may step out without warning.** Every tool you will meet later (quorums, leader election, consensus like Raft, sagas, retries) is a procedure for that room to keep agreeing anyway. If you remember that the network and the other machines cannot be trusted to be fast or alive, the rest of distributed systems is just clever ways to cope.

<Recap lang="en">
The moment your app spans more than one computer, you have a distributed system: separate nodes cooperating only by passing messages over a network. That buys scale, availability, and closeness — but costs you the single source of truth. Machines fail on their own, messages arrive late or not at all, and there is no shared clock, so nodes can never be certain who is alive. The entire field is procedures for reaching agreement anyway. Carry this picture up: a room passing notes that get lost, reordered, or duplicated, where anyone may leave without warning.
</Recap>
