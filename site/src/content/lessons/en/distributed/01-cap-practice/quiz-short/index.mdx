Returning stale data is still 'available'. Many marketed-as-available databases are CP because, under a partition, the minority side rejects requests to preserve linearizability — e.g. a Raft cluster (etcd, Consul) where the isolated minority refuses reads and writes. They choose correctness over responsiveness, which is the CP stance." },
    { q: "What does the PACELC theorem add over CAP, and what does choosing EC cost in the healthy state?", a: "CAP only describes behaviour during a partition, but networks are healthy ~99.9% of the time. PACELC (Abadi, 2012) adds the else-branch: if Partitioned, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. Choosing EC (consistency when healthy) means every read and write coordinates synchronously across replicas — quorum acknowledgement, often across datacenters — so you pay the network round-trip latency on every request. Choosing EL means asynchronous replication and eventual consistency, buying low latency at the cost of possibly serving stale data even with no partition. So strong consistency is never free: under partition it costs availability, and when healthy it costs latency." },
    { q: "Explain how a GC pause or CPU saturation can cause a 'logical' partition in a CP consensus cluster.", a: "A failure detector cannot tell 'slow' from 'dead' — it only sees missed heartbeats. If a node suffers a 10-second stop-the-world GC pause or has its CPU saturated by a background job, it stops answering heartbeats even though no network link was cut and the node is otherwise healthy. Its peers conclude it failed and trigger a leader re-election, blocking writes during the election and wasting the old leader's in-flight work. This is a logical partition. Raft does not depend on synchronized wall clocks (it uses logical terms and sequence numbers); the trigger is purely the missed heartbeats. Pre-vote and election timeouts tuned above worst-case pause/RTT mitigate the disruptive flapping." },
    { q: "You chose AP. What conflict-resolution tax do you owe, and why is Last-Write-Wins on wall-clock timestamps dangerous?", a: "Choosing AP means both sides of a partition accept writes independently, so the same key can diverge — e.g. a shopping cart edited in two regions. When the partition heals, you must reconcile those versions. Last-Write-Wins keeps the write with the highest timestamp, but timestamps come from node clocks, which drift. A genuinely-later write on a slightly-lagging clock can lose to an earlier write with a higher timestamp, so LWW silently and irreversibly drops legitimate customer data with no error. This is per-key strong consistency, not full linearizability. It interacts with CAP because reaching W or R replicas requires that many to be reachable: during a partition, a side that cannot assemble its quorum must fail the operation (CP behaviour). Lowering consistency (e.g. R=W=1) restores availability on a partitioned side but sacrifices the overlap guarantee, sliding the system toward AP.
