<Crux>A cache is a saved copy of an expensive answer kept close by, so you don't redo the work.</Crux>

<Explanation>

## Why keep a copy at all?

Some answers are slow or costly to produce. Reading a row from a database, fetching a page across the network, resizing a big image, running a long calculation — each takes time and effort. If the answer rarely changes, redoing that work for every request is waste. So you do the slow work once, keep the result somewhere quick to reach, and hand out that saved copy next time.

That saved copy is the **cache**. The whole idea rests on two facts being true often enough: the work is expensive, and the same answer gets asked for again. When both hold, a cache turns many slow trips into one slow trip plus many fast ones.

## Hit, miss, and the catch

Two words describe what happens when you look in the cache:

- A **hit** — the copy is there. You return it fast and skip the expensive work.
- A **miss** — the copy is not there (or has expired). You do the slow work, then save the result so the next ask is a hit.

The catch is that a copy can go out of date. The real answer might change while your saved copy stays the same — now the cache holds something **stale**. A copy that is too old can be worse than no copy, because it is wrong but looks confident. So the hard part of caching is never "save a copy"; it is deciding when a copy is still good enough to trust, and when to throw it away and do the work again.

## What this climb covers

This pillar is the craft of using copies well. You will learn where caches live (from the chip in your computer out to servers near your users), how long to keep a copy before it counts as stale, how browsers and servers agree on freshness, and what goes wrong when many requests miss at the same moment and all stampede the slow work at once. One mental model carries you through all of it: a cache is a saved copy of an expensive answer, and every rule exists to keep that copy fast and trustworthy.

<Recap lang="en">
A cache is a saved copy of an expensive answer kept close by, so repeated asks skip the slow work. A hit reuses the copy; a miss does the work and saves it. The price is staleness: a copy can drift out of date, so the real craft is keeping copies fast to reach and fresh enough to trust. The rest of this pillar covers where caches live, how long copies last, how freshness is agreed, and what breaks when everyone misses at once.
