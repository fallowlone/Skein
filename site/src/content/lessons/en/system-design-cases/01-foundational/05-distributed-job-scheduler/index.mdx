<Hook>
A billing company ran its monthly invoicing as a cron entry on one server. It worked for years. Then the server was replaced during a maintenance window that overlapped midnight on the first of the month, and invoicing simply didn't run. Nobody noticed for three days. The fix was to run the cron on two servers "for redundancy." The next month every customer got two invoices and two charges. The team had discovered both failure modes of scheduling in sixty days: a job that runs zero times, and a job that runs twice. A distributed scheduler exists to make the first impossible and the second harmless.
</Hook>

<Crux>A scheduler finds due jobs and leases them to workers. Losing a job is a bug; running one twice is a given, so jobs must be idempotent or fenced.</Crux>

<Explanation>

## Requirements

Functional:

- Submit a job: a payload (HTTP call, queue message, or task name with arguments) plus a schedule, either one-off ("at 2026-11-01 09:00 UTC") or recurring (a cron expression with a time zone).
- Execute each due job on a worker close to its scheduled time.
- Retry failed runs with backoff, up to a limit, then park them in a dead-letter state.
- Query status and history per job; pause, resume, and cancel jobs.

Non-functional:

- **No lost jobs.** A due job must eventually run even if schedulers or workers crash. This is the property the single cron server lacked.
- **At-least-once execution**, with duplicates rare and detectable. Exactly-once execution of an arbitrary side effect is not achievable; the design must say so out loud.
- **Trigger latency** under a few seconds at p99, including at peak.
- **High availability** of both the scheduling path and the API, and multi-tenant fairness so one tenant's burst doesn't starve others.

## Estimation

Assume 50M registered jobs and 10M executions per day.

- Average rate: 10M / 86,400 ≈ **115 executions/s**. Trivial on average.
- The real number is the peak. Humans write `0 * * * *` and `0 0 * * *`. If half of each hour's executions are scheduled for minute zero, that's 10M / 24 / 2 ≈ 208,000 jobs that all want to start in the same second. **The top of the hour is the design constraint**, not the daily average.
- Storage: 50M job definitions × ~1 KB ≈ 50 GB, plus run history at 10M rows/day (partition by day and expire after a retention window).

## High-level design

Separate *what should run* (job definitions) from *when it is due* (an index on next-run time) from *running it* (workers pulling from a queue). Each stage can scale and fail independently.

On each dispatch the scheduler inserts a run row (`job_id`, `scheduled_for`, `attempt`), enqueues it, and advances the job's `next_run_at` to the next cron occurrence, all inside one transaction or with an outbox, so a crash between "enqueued" and "advanced" can't silently skip or double-dispatch.

## Deep dive

### Finding due jobs without two schedulers grabbing the same one

The naive loop is `SELECT … WHERE next_run_at <= now()`. Run it on two scheduler instances for availability and both pick the same rows: the double-invoice bug again. Three standard fixes:

- **Row locking with skip.** `SELECT … WHERE next_run_at <= now() ORDER BY next_run_at LIMIT 500 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`. Each instance locks a batch; others skip locked rows instead of blocking. Simple, correct, and fine up to thousands of jobs per second on one Postgres primary.
- **Partition ownership.** Hash `job_id` into N partitions; each scheduler shard holds a lease on a set of partitions (via etcd, ZooKeeper, or a lease table) and is the only one scanning them. Scales horizontally; rebalancing on shard death is leader election per partition.
- **Time buckets.** Keep due jobs in per-minute buckets (a Redis sorted set scored by timestamp, or a hierarchical timing wheel in memory), so finding what's due is reading one bucket rather than range-scanning a 50M-row index.

### Leases, heartbeats, and why duplicates are guaranteed

A worker that pulls a run takes a **lease**: the run is invisible to other workers until the lease expires (SQS calls this the visibility timeout). Long jobs heartbeat to extend the lease. If the worker crashes, the lease lapses and the run is redelivered: that is how "no lost jobs" is achieved.

The same mechanism guarantees duplicates. A worker can be *alive but paused* (a 40-second GC pause, a network partition) while its 30-second lease expires. A second worker takes the run and executes it. Then the first worker wakes up and finishes too. No lease length fixes this, for the same reason no failover timeout distinguishes "dead" from "slow."

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
Why not just use a distributed lock so only one worker runs a job? Because a lock is a lease with a timeout, and a paused holder doesn't know its lease expired. Martin Kleppmann's analysis of Redis-based locking shows the general problem: any lock that can expire can be held by two clients at once from the clients' point of view. The fix moves to the *resource*: every run carries a **fencing token**, a monotonically increasing number such as the attempt counter, and the side effect rejects writes with a token lower than one it has already seen. Where you can't fence the resource (an external email API), you pass an **idempotency key** such as `job_id + scheduled_for`, so the provider deduplicates. The scheduler guarantees at-least-once; the job makes it effectively-once.
</Inset>

### Recurring schedules: time zones, DST, and misfires

Computing the next run time is harder than parsing five cron fields:

- **Time zones and DST.** "Every day at 02:30 Europe/Berlin" has no 02:30 on the spring-forward day and two of them on the fall-back day. Store the schedule with its IANA time zone, compute the next occurrence in that zone, store it as UTC, and pick an explicit rule for the skipped and repeated hour.
- **Misfire policy.** If the scheduler was down for three hours, an hourly job missed three runs. The job declares what to do: *fire once now* (most jobs), *fire every missed run* (billing periods, each of which matters), or *skip to the next* (a cache warmer). Quartz and Kubernetes CronJobs expose exactly these choices.
- **Overlap policy.** If the 10:00 run is still going at 11:00, do you start another, skip, or queue it? Kubernetes calls this `concurrencyPolicy: Allow | Forbid | Replace`.

### The top of the hour

Two hundred thousand jobs due in the same second will overrun any queue and any downstream API they call. Defenses, in order of impact:

- **Jitter at creation.** When a user writes `0 * * * *` and doesn't need exact timing, the system silently spreads the job across a window (Jenkins' `H` syntax does this by hashing the job name). This flattens the spike before it exists.
- **Dispatch rate limits and priority queues.** Latency-sensitive jobs go to a high-priority queue; bulk reports wait.
- **Per-tenant quotas.** Fair scheduling so one tenant with 100k hourly jobs can't consume every worker at :00.

## Bottlenecks & tradeoffs

- **The jobs index** is the hot spot: partition it by job hash and by time bucket before scanning 50M rows every second becomes the ceiling.
- **Exactly-once is a property of the job, not the scheduler.** Publish this in the API contract: every run has a stable `run_id` and `attempt` number; jobs must be idempotent on `run_id`.
- **Trigger precision vs. load:** sub-second precision at the top of the hour costs enormous peak capacity. Most jobs don't need it; make precision opt-in and jitter the default.
- **Push vs. pull to workers:** a queue with leases (pull) gives back-pressure and survives worker crashes; pushing HTTP calls directly from the scheduler couples dispatch latency to the slowest job.

</Explanation>

<KeyTakeaway>Store next_run_at, let one owner per partition dispatch, lease runs to workers. Duplicates are guaranteed, so fence or key every side effect. Plan for the top of the hour, not the average.</KeyTakeaway>

<Recap lang="en">
A distributed job scheduler exists to rule out both failures from the hook: the job that **runs zero times** (a single cron host that was down) and the job that **runs twice** (two cron hosts "for redundancy"). The design separates **job definitions** with a `next_run_at`, **scheduler shards** that turn due jobs into queued runs, and **workers** that execute under a **lease**. Finding due jobs safely uses `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` at small scale and **partition ownership with leases** or **time buckets** at large scale, and each dispatch records a run and advances the schedule atomically. Leases make lost jobs impossible, because an expired lease redelivers, and for the same reason make **duplicates inevitable**: a paused worker outlives its lease. So the scheduler promises **at-least-once**, and each job becomes effectively-once with a **fencing token** or an **idempotency key** at the side effect. Recurring schedules need IANA time zones with explicit **DST** rules, a per-job **misfire policy**, and an **overlap policy**. The real capacity constraint is the **top of the hour**, handled with jitter, priority queues, and per-tenant quotas. With these in place, the billing team's monthly run happens exactly when a lease is held, and a second attempt hits an idempotency key instead of a customer's card.
</Recap>
