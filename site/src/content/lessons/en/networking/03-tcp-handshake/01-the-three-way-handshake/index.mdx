## What TCP does in one sentence

TCP turns two strangers on the network into a reliable two-way conversation: it numbers the byte stream, uses acknowledgements to confirm how much has arrived, and retransmits missing data. The handshake is how that conversation starts.

IP moves packets across networks, but it does not promise that they arrive, arrive once, or arrive in order. TCP adds the machinery that applications usually want: ordered delivery, retransmission, flow control, and a shared notion of where each direction of the byte stream begins. Before the handshake, the endpoints have no synchronized TCP connection. After it, both carry connection-specific state.

## Why care

Without TCP every web request would have to manage its own retries and ordering. TCP gives every higher protocol (HTTP, IMAP, SSH, anything that runs over it) a clean reliable pipe for free. When you see an unexplained connection delay or a latency spike on a fresh request, there is a good chance the handshake cost is the culprit — understanding its structure tells you exactly where to look.

## One round trip to establish the connection

The handshake consumes one network round trip. On a local link that can disappear into the noise; across oceans or a mobile network it becomes visible immediately. The important detail is that the third ACK may already carry application data, so the setup cost is one RTT rather than an extra empty exchange after it.

## From a phone call to the wire

Imagine two people on a phone call. Before they say anything important they exchange "hello — can you hear me? — yes, I can hear you, can you hear me? — yes." That short three-step ritual is the TCP handshake.

- **SYN** = "hello, here is my counter"
- **SYN-ACK** = "I hear you, here is my counter, and I confirm I heard your counter"
- **ACK** = "I confirm your counter, let's begin"

The metaphor is useful only if we can map it back to the protocol. Here is the same conversation expressed as human intent, protocol meaning, and exact wire fields.

## Why three steps and not two

TCP is bidirectional and **both sides must synchronize each other's starting sequence numbers.** After only SYN and SYN-ACK, the client knows the server received `x` and the client has learned `y`. The server still has no proof that its own `y` reached the client. The third ACK closes that knowledge gap.

## One scenario end to end

You click a link in your browser. DNS resolves the hostname to an IP address. The operating system routes toward that address and performs the TCP handshake you just learned. For HTTPS, TLS then establishes encryption and authenticates the peer; after that, HTTP carries the request. For plain HTTP, the request can begin as soon as TCP is ready.

## Where the reliability actually lives

Routers forward IP packets without keeping a TCP connection record for every flow. The reliability machinery lives at the endpoints: sequence tracking, retransmission timers, receive/send buffers, and the TCP control block that ties those pieces to one connection.

<StatefulEndpointFig lang="en" span="learning" />

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
**Why TCP adds statefulness.** IP is stateless: each packet is routed independently and the network keeps no memory of previous packets. TCP adds state at both endpoints — buffers, window sizes, sequence counters. This state is what enables reliability. The handshake is the moment that state is created and agreed upon. After it, both sides are no longer strangers.
</Inset>

<Recap lang="en">
TCP builds a reliable ordered byte stream on top of IP's best-effort delivery. The client proposes an initial sequence number with SYN, the server acknowledges it and proposes its own with SYN-ACK, and the client confirms the server's number with ACK. The third message exists because the server needs proof that its own starting point reached the client. Connection setup therefore costs one RTT; the final ACK can already carry application data. When a fresh connection feels slow, measure the RTT first — geography may be the dominant part of the setup cost.
</Recap>
