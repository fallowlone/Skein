<Hook>
A bug report lands: two customers received the same invoice number. The on-call engineer replies in one sentence: "Impossible — invoice numbers come from a column with a unique index, so if two rows had the same number, the database would have rejected the second insert." Look at the shape of that sentence. She *assumed the report was true*, followed the system's guarantees to an impossibility, and concluded the report must be wrong. That is a proof by contradiction — the second-oldest move in mathematics, the one that felled √2 and crowned the primes infinite. The end of this particular story is worth keeping too: the report was right. A migration three weeks earlier had silently dropped the index. The engineer's *proof* was valid — but one of its "known facts" was no longer a fact. Both halves of the story — the move itself, and the way it fails — are this lesson.

<Crux>To prove a claim by contradiction, assume it is false, derive something impossible, and conclude the claim was true all along.</Crux>

<Explanation>

## The shape: assume false, hit a wall, conclude true

Why reach for contradiction at all? Because some claims are shaped so that the direct road — assume P, walk to Q — has nowhere to start. When the claim says "no such X exists" or "there is exactly one Y", assuming the opposite hands you a concrete object to interrogate. That gift is what makes the technique powerful.

A **proof by contradiction** of a claim C runs in three beats:

1. **Assume ¬C** — suppose, for the sake of argument, that the claim is false.
2. **Derive an impossibility** — using only valid steps, arrive at something that cannot be true: a statement that contradicts a known fact, or contradicts the assumption itself.
3. **Conclude C** — the assumption led to the impossible, so the assumption cannot stand; C holds.

Together these three beats mean: you turn the claim inside out, follow it to its logical endpoint, and let the system's own rules destroy it. Without step 2 being airtight — bedrock at every link except the assumption — step 3 proves nothing, because the blame could land anywhere.

Why is this legitimate? Because of the one law everything in this track rests on: **valid steps never lead from truth to falsehood**. If a chain of valid steps ends at something false, then at least one of its inputs was false. In a contradiction proof you engineer the chain so that *every* input except one is bedrock — definitions, established theorems, explicit guarantees. The single non-bedrock input is the assumption ¬C. When the wall is hit, the blame has exactly one place to land.

<Inset kind="why" lang="en">
Why does the assumption take the blame, and not some other statement? Audit the chain: every other link is either a definition (which cannot be false — it just fixes a word's meaning), a previously proven fact, or a valid inference from earlier links. Falsehood cannot originate in any of those. The only statement admitted without credentials was the assumption — so the falsehood at the end must trace back to it. Now invert this for production systems, where *nothing* is truly bedrock: the unique index can be dropped, the clock can jump, the "single writer" can become two. When the impossible happens in a live system, the correct conclusion is not "the report is wrong" but "one of my axioms quietly stopped being an axiom" — and the postmortem is the hunt for which one.
</Inset>

## Classic one: √2 is irrational

A number is **rational** if it can be written as a fraction p/q of integers with q ≠ 0 — and any such fraction can be reduced to **lowest terms**, where p and q share no common factor. The claim: √2 is not such a number. Notice the claim's shape — *there is no fraction equal to √2*. How would you attack that directly? Check every fraction? Contradiction hands you a way in: assume the fraction exists, and interrogate it.

**Claim.** √2 is irrational.

**Proof.** Assume not: √2 = p/q for integers p, q with q ≠ 0, the fraction in lowest terms. Squaring both sides gives 2 = p²/q², so p² = 2q². Thus p² is even. From the previous lesson: if p were odd, then p² would be odd — p² is even, so by contraposition p is even. Write p = 2k. Substituting: (2k)² = 2q², so 4k² = 2q², so q² = 2k². Thus q² is even, and by the same contraposition, q is even. Now p and q are both even — both divisible by 2 — yet the fraction was taken in lowest terms. That is the impossibility. Every step after the assumption was plain algebra and an already-proven fact, so the assumption is to blame: no such fraction exists. √2 is irrational. ∎

Slow down on two details. First, "lowest terms" was not decoration — it is the known fact the wall is made of. Second, see the cameo: the contrapositive from lesson one did the heavy lifting *inside* the contradiction proof. Techniques compose; a contradiction proof is usually a direct chain wearing a different opening move.

## Classic two: there is no largest prime

Another impossibility claim, twenty-three centuries old. A **prime** is an integer greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself; a known fact we lean on: every integer greater than 1 has at least one prime factor.

**Claim.** There are infinitely many primes.

**Proof.** Assume not: there are finitely many, and the complete list is p₁, p₂, …, pₙ. Build the number N = p₁·p₂·…·pₙ + 1. Divide N by any prime pᵢ on the list: it divides the product part exactly, so the division leaves remainder 1. Hence no prime on the list divides N. But N is greater than 1, so it has *some* prime factor — which therefore is not on the list. The list claimed to contain every prime; here is a prime it missed. Impossible. So no finite list can contain all primes. ∎

One trap to disarm, because it is the most misremembered step in all of mathematics: the proof does **not** claim N itself is prime. It claims N has a prime factor outside the list — N might be that prime, or might factor into new primes. Concretely: 2·3·5·7·11·13 + 1 = 30031 = 59·509 — not prime, but its factors 59 and 509 are new. The contradiction needs only "a prime not on the list", and that, the remainder argument delivers.

## Where contradiction shines: impossibility and uniqueness

Both classics share a shape: *there is no X* — no fraction for √2, no largest prime. Such claims are miserable to attack directly, because "checking all candidates" never ends. Contradiction flips the misery into a gift: assume the claim false, and you are *handed a concrete object* — the fraction, the finite list — to interrogate with your existing tools. The same gift appears with **uniqueness** claims ("at most one X"): assume two exist, and you have two named things to play off each other.

Engineers run these arguments constantly, mostly without noticing. Termination is an impossibility claim — *there is no infinite run*:

```js
function drainQueue(queue) {
  // Claim: this loop always terminates.
  while (queue.length > 0) {
    queue.pop(); // every pass removes exactly one element
  }
}
// Assume it loops forever. Each pass strictly decreases queue.length by 1,
// so the length would decrease forever. But a length is a non-negative
// integer — it cannot decrease forever. Impossible → the loop terminates.
```

And the invoice story is a uniqueness argument from system guarantees: assume two rows share an invoice number; the second `INSERT` would have violated the unique index; the database rejects violating inserts; yet both rows exist — impossible, *given the index exists*. Spelled out this way, the argument is honest about its bedrock: atomic single-statement updates, a unique index that is actually present, one database rather than two replicas accepting writes. Each "known fact" is a load-bearing link.

## The discipline: the wall must come from the assumption

Here is the failure mode that produces false proofs. In a contradiction proof, *every* link except the assumption must be airtight — because the technique's whole logic is "the blame has only one place to land". Make one arithmetic slip mid-chain, and you will still happily arrive at an "impossibility"; the proof will look finished and prove nothing, because now the falsehood has two possible sources and you picked the flattering one. A contradiction at the end does not certify the chain; it indicts *some* input, and only a clean chain entitles you to point at the assumption.

This is precisely what bit the on-call engineer in the opening story. Her chain was valid; her conclusion — "the report is wrong" — followed only if the unique index existed, and it did not. The mathematical discipline and the operational one are the same rule read in two directions: in a proof, scrub every link until the assumption is the only suspect; in an incident, when the impossible has visibly happened, re-audit your "known facts" until you find the one that lied.

## Contradiction or contrapositive?

The two get conflated because both begin by writing a ¬ somewhere. Keep the scopes straight: the **contrapositive** proves only implications — it transforms P→Q into ¬Q→¬P, and if the claim is not an implication, there is nothing to transform. **Contradiction** is shape-agnostic: "√2 is irrational" and "there is no largest prime" are not implications, and contradiction handled both.

But when the claim *is* an implication, watch for a common disguise. To prove P→Q by contradiction you assume P ∧ ¬Q and hunt an impossibility. Very often the hunt uses only ¬Q: you derive ¬P from it, declare "contradiction with P!", and stop. Look at what you actually built — a chain from ¬Q to ¬P. That *is* the contrapositive proof; the assumption P sat in the room contributing nothing but the final collision. When that happens, restate the proof as a contrapositive: one live assumption instead of two, less for the reader to track, and the proof announces what it actually did. Reserve genuine contradiction for when the derivation materially uses *both* P and ¬Q — then the extra assumption is earning its keep.

<Recap lang="en">
Proof by contradiction runs in three beats: assume the claim is false, derive something impossible by valid steps, conclude the claim is true. The engine underneath is validity itself — valid steps never carry truth into falsehood, so a chain that ends at the impossible had a false input, and if every link except the assumption is bedrock, the assumption is the only suspect. The two classics show the craft: assume √2 = p/q in lowest terms, square, and watch evenness infect p and then q until "no common factor" collapses; assume a complete finite list of primes, multiply them all and add 1, and the remainder of 1 manufactures a prime the list missed — noting carefully that N itself need not be prime, only its factors new. The technique shines exactly where direct attack drowns: impossibility and uniqueness claims, where assuming falsehood hands you a concrete object to interrogate — a fraction, a list, two duplicate invoice rows, an infinite run of a loop whose counter cannot decrease forever. Two disciplines keep it honest. The wall must trace to the assumption: one arithmetic slip gives blame a second home and the proof proves nothing — and in production, where no fact is truly bedrock, the impossible happening means an axiom quietly died; go find which. And when your contradiction proof of an implication only ever used ¬Q, it is a contrapositive in disguise — restate it, and let the proof say what it did. Now when you see an engineer declare a bug "impossible from our guarantees", you will know to audit every link in the chain — and to ask which "known fact" might have quietly stopped being one.
</Recap>
