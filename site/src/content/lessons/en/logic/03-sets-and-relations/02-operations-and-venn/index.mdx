## Four operations, one universe

Take two sets over the same kind of values — say `A = {1, 2, 3}` and `B = {3, 4}`. There are exactly four classic ways to combine them, and each is defined by a membership rule:

- **Union** A ∪ B — members in A *or* in B (or both): `{1, 2, 3, 4}`.
- **Intersection** A ∩ B — members in A *and* in B: `{3}`.
- **Difference** A ∖ B — members of A that are *not* in B: `{1, 2}`. Note the direction: B ∖ A is `{4}`, a different set.
- **Complement** — everything *not* in A. This one comes with a condition: "everything" must be declared first. Outside the set of your users, what is there — other people? numbers? teapots? A complement only makes sense inside a stated **universe** U, the set of everything under discussion. If U is all registered accounts, the complement of "subscribed accounts" is precisely "registered accounts that are not subscribed". No universe, no complement.

Each definition above is a membership *rule*, and the rules are built from the connectives you already know from propositional logic:

- x ∈ A ∪ B means x ∈ A **or** x ∈ B,
- x ∈ A ∩ B means x ∈ A **and** x ∈ B,
- x ∈ A ∖ B means x ∈ A **and not** x ∈ B,
- x is in the complement of A means **not** x ∈ A.

This is the deepest fact in the lesson: set operations are not a new theory. They are OR, AND and NOT applied to membership statements. Every law you learned about connectives transfers to sets for free.

```python
a = {1, 2, 3}
b = {3, 4}

a | b   # union        → {1, 2, 3, 4}   ("or")
a & b   # intersection → {3}            ("and")
a - b   # difference   → {1, 2}         ("and not")
b - a   # the other direction           → {4}
```

And the same operations appear wherever a condition selects rows. A SQL `WHERE active AND beta` returns the intersection of the active set and the beta set; `filter` with two predicates joined by `&&` is an intersection computed one membership test at a time:

```js
const inBoth = users.filter((u) => isActive(u) && isBeta(u));   // A ∩ B
const inEither = users.filter((u) => isActive(u) || isBeta(u)); // A ∪ B
const activeOnly = users.filter((u) => isActive(u) && !isBeta(u)); // A ∖ B
```

## Venn diagrams: regions you can point at

A **Venn diagram** draws each set as a circle inside a rectangle (the universe). Two overlapping circles cut the rectangle into exactly four regions, and every value of U lives in exactly one of them: in A only, in both, in B only, in neither. Each operation is then a *selection of regions* — union shades three regions, intersection shades the middle lens, difference A ∖ B shades the left crescent, and the complement of A ∪ B shades only the outside.

Read a Venn diagram the way you read a map: do not ask "what does ∩ mean" in the abstract — ask *which regions are shaded*. With three circles the rectangle splits into eight regions (every combination of in/out for three sets), and questions like "in A and B but not C" stop being word puzzles: they are one region you can put a finger on.

## De Morgan, now with regions

Propositional logic gave you De Morgan's laws: not (P or Q) is the same as (not P) and (not Q). Substitute membership statements for P and Q and the law becomes a fact about sets — the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements:

- *not in (A ∪ B)* — outside both circles — equals *not in A* **and** *not in B*.
- *not in (A ∩ B)* — outside the lens — equals *not in A* **or** *not in B*.

Now the Hook bug is one line of algebra. Wanted: the complement of (unsubscribed ∪ bounced) — users in *neither* bad set. De Morgan says that is (not unsubscribed) ∩ (not bounced) — an AND of two negations:

```js
// Wanted: NOT (unsubscribed OR bounced)
// De Morgan: (NOT unsubscribed) AND (NOT bounced)
const eligible = users.filter((u) => !u.unsubscribed && !u.bounced); // correct

// Shipped: (NOT unsubscribed) OR (NOT bounced)
// — true for anyone missing from at least ONE bad set,
//   which includes a bounced user who never unsubscribed.
const buggy = users.filter((u) => !u.unsubscribed || !u.bounced);
```

On the Venn map, the correct set is the single outside region. The shipped condition shades everything except the lens — three regions instead of one, and the two extra regions are exactly the people who got the email and should not have.

<Inset kind="mistake" lang="en">
The reliable way to catch a De Morgan slip in review: take the suspicious condition and test it against one concrete member of each Venn region — a user who is only unsubscribed, one who only bounced, one who is both, one who is neither. Four test values, four regions, and a wrong connective always misclassifies at least one of them. This is faster and more convincing than re-deriving the algebra in your head, and it works on conditions far messier than two flags.
</Inset>

## Counting a union: inclusion-exclusion

The newsletter has 1,200 subscribers and the beta program has 300 members. How big is the combined audience? Not 1,500 — unless no one is in both. Anyone in the lens was counted twice, once per circle, so subtract the overlap once:

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|

With 100 people in both: 1,200 + 300 − 100 = **1,400** real humans. This is **inclusion-exclusion**, and the Venn map makes it visible: |A| + |B| covers the lens twice, so one subtraction restores every region to being counted exactly once.

```python
newsletter = load_newsletter_ids()   # 1200 ids
beta = load_beta_ids()               # 300 ids

audience = newsletter | beta
len(audience)                        # 1400 — not 1500
len(newsletter) + len(beta) - len(newsletter & beta)  # 1400, same number
```

If you have ever seen a dashboard where segment totals add up to more than the user count, you have seen inclusion-exclusion being ignored in production.

## The misconception: "difference works both ways"

Subtraction of numbers already warned you: 5 − 3 is not 3 − 5. Set difference is just as directional, and the symmetry assumption sneaks into real code as "the diff of two lists". With `A = {1, 2, 3}` and `B = {3, 4}`: A ∖ B is `{1, 2}` (what deploying would remove), while B ∖ A is `{4}` (what deploying would add) — different sets answering different questions. When you genuinely want "elements in exactly one of the two", that is its own operation, the **symmetric difference** A △ B = `{1, 2, 4}`:

```python
a = {1, 2, 3}
b = {3, 4}

a - b   # {1, 2}     — directional: in a, not in b
b - a   # {4}        — the other direction
a ^ b   # {1, 2, 4}  — symmetric difference: in exactly one
```

A config-sync tool that computes only `current - desired` will happily delete obsolete keys and never add missing ones. Ask which direction you mean — or whether you mean both.

<Recap lang="en">
Four operations combine sets, and each is a logic connective acting on membership: union A ∪ B is OR, intersection A ∩ B is AND, difference A ∖ B is AND-NOT, and the complement is NOT — legal only inside a declared universe U, because "everything not in A" is meaningless until "everything" is a set. Because the operations are connectives, De Morgan transfers verbatim: the complement of A ∪ B is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of A ∩ B is the union of the complements — which is exactly the law the buggy filter `!u.unsubscribed || !u.bounced` violated; the wanted region was the single outside of the union, the shipped condition shaded three regions. A Venn diagram is the map that makes such bugs visible: two circles in a rectangle cut the universe into four regions, three circles into eight, every value lives in exactly one region, and every operation is a selection of regions you can point at. Counting follows the map too: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|, because summing circle sizes counts the lens twice — 1,200 newsletter plus 300 beta with 100 in both is 1,400 people, not 1,500. And difference is directional: A ∖ B and B ∖ A answer different questions, while "in exactly one" is its own operation, the symmetric difference A △ B. In code: `a | b`, `a & b`, `a - b`, `a ^ b` in Python; predicates joined by `&&`, `||`, `!` in a filter; AND in a WHERE clause — the same algebra at every layer.
</Recap>
