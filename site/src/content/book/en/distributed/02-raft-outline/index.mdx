CAP in practice (<a href="/en/distributed/01-cap-practice/">01-cap-practice</a>) told us we cannot have consistency, availability, and partition tolerance at the same time. CP systems — the ones that pick consistency under partition — still have to agree on one history of changes across many machines. Raft is how that agreement is reached in etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, MongoDB config servers, Kafka KRaft, and roughly every other production CP store you have ever touched.

<Crux>How three to seven machines, any of which can crash or be partitioned at any time, agree on exactly one ordered log of changes.</Crux>

The job of consensus is to make a cluster of N machines behave like one machine. That means every change must be ordered the same way on every survivor, every reader must see committed changes, and no acknowledged change can ever be lost. Raft does this by electing a single leader at a time, letting only the leader add to a log, and replicating that log to a quorum of followers before treating each entry as committed. The trick is in the edge cases — what happens when the leader dies, the network splits, the disk is full, or a node has been offline for hours. The next sections unpack each of these.

    <p><strong>What Raft does in one sentence.</strong> It lets a small group of computers agree on one history of changes even if some of them crash or briefly disconnect.</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Without it, two halves of a split cluster would each accept conflicting writes and your system would end up with two versions of the truth.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> A village council of five members decides laws. One member holds the gavel — the speaker. New laws come to the speaker, who shouts each to the others and waits for two more "got it" replies before declaring it passed. If the speaker goes quiet, the rest elect a new one — three votes (majority of five) wins. Three of five guarantees that two speakers cannot exist at once — not enough villagers for two majorities.</p>
    <p><strong>Sven and Otto.</strong> <PersonaTag id="sven" lang="en" /> is leader of a five-node cluster. <PersonaTag id="otto" lang="en" /> is a follower. Every client write: Sven appends to his log, shouts it out. Otto writes it, replies "got it". After two more confirmations, change is final. If Sven crashes, others time out and elect a new leader.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario.</strong> Five etcd nodes back Kubernetes. <code>kubectl apply</code> writes the manifest into etcd. The Raft leader replicates it to the others; once three have it on disk, etcd answers "stored". If the leader's host loses network, the rest pick a new leader — your deployment never stalls.</p>

  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p><strong>Three roles, one term.</strong> Every node in a Raft cluster is in one of three states at any time: <em>follower</em>, <em>candidate</em>, or <em>leader</em>. A node starts as a follower. If it stops hearing from a leader for an election timeout (typically randomised between 150-300 ms), it becomes a candidate, increments its current <em>term</em> (a monotonic logical clock per cluster), votes for itself, and asks the other nodes for their votes via the <code>RequestVote</code> RPC. If it collects votes from a majority of the cluster, it becomes leader for that term and starts sending heartbeats. If two candidates split the vote, the term ends with no leader and both fall back to follower for a fresh randomised timeout. The randomisation is what breaks ties: with a wide enough range, the probability of repeated split votes drops fast.</p>
    <p><strong>The election timeout and heartbeats.</strong> A leader sends an <code>AppendEntries</code> RPC to every follower at a regular interval shorter than the election timeout — typically 50 ms heartbeats with 150-300 ms election timeout. The heartbeat carries the leader's current term, its commit index, and any new log entries (or none, in which case it is a pure liveness signal). A follower that receives a heartbeat with a term at least its own resets its election timer and acknowledges. A leader whose heartbeat is rejected (because the receiver has seen a higher term) immediately steps down to follower; the higher term wins. This term comparison is the entire deduplication layer for "who is the real leader right now."</p>
    <p><strong>The log and AppendEntries.</strong> The leader keeps an in-memory + on-disk log of entries, each a tuple (term, index, command). When a client sends a write, the leader appends a new entry to its own log first, then sends <code>AppendEntries</code> to every follower with the new entry plus the term and index of the previous entry. The follower checks: do I have an entry at that previous index with that previous term? If yes, append the new entry and reply success. If no, reply mismatch — the leader will then back up one index and retry, eventually finding the last point of agreement and overwriting the follower's divergent tail with its own. This back-and-forth is rare in steady state (followers are usually one entry behind, not many), and amortises to almost zero in production.</p>
    <p><strong>Commit, apply, and the state machine.</strong> A log entry is <em>committed</em> when the leader has heard from a majority of nodes (including itself) that they have stored the entry on disk. The leader's commit index is the highest index known to be committed. The leader piggybacks its commit index on the next AppendEntries, so followers learn within one round-trip which entries they can now apply to their state machine. The state machine is application-specific — it could be a key-value map, a relational table, a config tree — but Raft does not look at it. It just says: every node, in log order, applies every committed entry exactly once, and the state machine is therefore deterministic across replicas. This is the entire mechanism: ordered replicated log + deterministic state machine = replicated state machine.</p>
    <p><strong>Why a majority for commits AND elections.</strong> The safety proof of Raft turns on quorum overlap. Any two majorities of a set of N must share at least one element (pigeonhole: twice the ceiling of (N+1)/2 exceeds N). When a new leader is elected for term T+1, its election quorum overlaps with the commit quorum of every committed entry of every prior term. Through that shared node, the new leader is guaranteed to see every previously committed entry (Raft's election rule rejects candidates whose log is behind any voter's log). So no committed entry can be lost across a leader transition. This is the property called <em>Leader Completeness</em>, and it is the whole reason Raft is safe even when leaders die mid-term.</p>
    <p><strong>The four safety properties.</strong> Raft's correctness proof reduces to four invariants. <strong>Election Safety</strong>: at most one leader per term (followed from "one vote per node per term + majority required"). <strong>Leader Append-Only</strong>: a leader never overwrites or deletes its own log entries — it only appends. <strong>Log Matching</strong>: if two logs share an entry at index i with term t, they are identical for all indices up to i (followed from the AppendEntries consistency check). <strong>Leader Completeness</strong>: any entry committed in some term is in the log of every leader of higher terms. <strong>State Machine Safety</strong> (derived from the above): no two nodes apply different commands at the same index. Memorise these and you have the entire mental model.</p>
    <p><strong>Where Raft does not apply.</strong> Raft is a crash-fault-tolerant (CFT) algorithm — it assumes nodes either follow the protocol or stop. It does not tolerate Byzantine faults (nodes lying, sending corrupted messages, or being adversarial). Byzantine workloads need PBFT, HotStuff, Tendermint, or other BFT consensus. For practically every backend database, config store, and cluster manager, CFT is sufficient because the nodes are inside your own datacenter and the threat model is hardware/software crashes, not malicious operators. Choose BFT only when you actually have adversarial participants — typical of blockchains and some federated systems.</p>
    <p><strong>Practical wire shape and operations.</strong> An etcd or Consul cluster spends 90% of its life in the steady state: leader sends heartbeats every 50 ms, followers acknowledge, occasional client writes get committed in 2-3 ms intra-region. Leader elections happen on every restart, network blip, or process pause longer than the election timeout. On a healthy cluster, elections complete in 100-500 ms; the cluster is briefly unavailable for writes during that window. The operational metrics worth tracking: leader-change frequency, commit latency p50/p99, heartbeat round-trip times, and disk-fsync latency on the leader (every committed entry has to be on disk before acknowledgement, so a slow disk on the leader is the most common cause of high tail latency).</p>
    <p><strong>Client routing and request redirection.</strong> Clients of a Raft cluster need to send writes to the current leader — only the leader can append to the log. The simplest pattern is "any node will redirect": a follower that receives a write replies with the address of the current leader (which it knows from recent heartbeats). The client retries against the redirect target. Some clients keep a small cache of "last known leader" to skip the redirect on subsequent writes. Etcd, Consul, and TiKV all implement this; in practice the redirect adds 1-5 ms on the rare miss, and steady-state writes go directly to the leader. Reads are different: linearizable reads must go through the leader's ReadIndex or lease path; eventually-consistent reads can be served by any follower. The application layer picks read consistency per query.</p>
    <p><strong>What goes wrong in practice — three failure modes.</strong> First, slow disk: if the leader's fsync starts taking longer than the heartbeat interval, followers time out before the leader can acknowledge their AppendEntries, and the cluster begins an election. The new leader hits the same disk and the cycle repeats. The fix is dedicated NVMe for the WAL, never shared cloud volumes. Second, network jitter: a brief partition or congestion event drops heartbeats and triggers an election, even though the cluster is mostly healthy. Pre-vote (see senior tier) mitigates this. Third, clock drift on lease reads: if the leader's clock drifts ahead of the followers, it can over-extend its lease window past the actual heartbeat round, serving reads that have lost the lease — a stale read. NTP-syncing all nodes is a correctness practice, not a hygiene one.</p>
    <p><strong>What Raft does NOT solve.</strong> Raft replicates a log. It does not give you a queryable database, a partitioning scheme, multi-key transactions, secondary indexes, geo-replication semantics, or schema migration. All of those live on top of Raft as application logic. A common newcomer mistake is to assume "we use Raft, so we have a distributed database"; the truth is "we use Raft to replicate one log; the database is the state machine we build above it". Build for the state machine you want; let Raft handle the consensus on changes to that state machine, and nothing more.</p>
    <p><strong>Minimal example: a KV store on top of Raft.</strong> The state machine is an in-memory hash map. Log entries are commands like (SET key value) or (DELETE key). When a client sends SET via the leader, the leader appends the entry, replicates to a majority, and once committed each node in log order applies the command to its hash map. Result: every node has an identical map after applying the same log. Reads can go through the leader for linearizability or through any follower for eventual consistency. That is the entire skeleton: build the state machine, write commands, let Raft replicate a consistent log. Most of the complexity in production systems is in the state machine, not in Raft itself.</p>

  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p><strong>Membership change via joint consensus.</strong> Adding or removing nodes from a running Raft cluster is the most error-prone operation in production. The naive "just swap the config and restart" can elect two leaders simultaneously during the transition — exactly the safety violation Raft otherwise prevents. The Raft paper section 6 defines <em>joint consensus</em>: switch through an intermediate configuration C_old_new where commits require a majority from BOTH the old and the new configuration. Once C_old_new is committed, the cluster transitions to C_new (majority of new only). The intermediate phase eliminates the window in which two different majorities could exist. Hashicorp Raft and etcd both implement this with single-server changes added on top (only add or remove one node at a time, with joint consensus underneath, to make the operational story simpler). The Alibaba Cloud writeup on Raft engineering practices documents real-world bugs that arose from skipping joint consensus in early implementations.</p>
    <p><strong>Pre-vote: avoiding term inflation.</strong> A node that rejoins after a long partition with a stale term can briefly look like a candidate at a very high term (because it kept incrementing during the partition). When its RequestVote arrives at healthy followers, they update their terms to match it, then realise its log is behind, deny the vote, and force the current leader to step down for nothing. The cluster has a leader election it did not need. Diego Ongaro's PhD thesis added the <em>pre-vote</em> phase: before incrementing its term and starting a real election, a candidate sends a non-binding pre-vote that other nodes evaluate; only if a majority would have voted yes does the candidate then actually increment and run a real election. Pre-vote is now standard in etcd, TiKV, and Hashicorp Raft. The cost is one extra RPC round in elections that would have succeeded anyway; the benefit is no spurious leader changes after partition healing.</p>
    <p><strong>Learners (non-voting members).</strong> Production systems regularly want to add a new node to a cluster, let it catch up its log over many minutes, and only then promote it to voter. Adding it directly as a voter would either require an immediate quorum that includes a node with an empty log (slows commits) or refuse writes during the catch-up. The <em>learner</em> extension makes a new node a non-voting receiver of AppendEntries: it follows the log but does not count toward quorums or vote in elections. Once its log catches up to within a small lag, the operator promotes it via a single-server membership change. Etcd, Consul, and Yugabyte all support learners. This is the safe way to scale cluster size or replace a dead node without dropping availability.</p>
    <p><strong>Linearizable reads: ReadIndex and lease reads.</strong> Strict linearizability requires that a read observes every previously committed write. Naive implementation: route every read through the leader, commit a no-op log entry just for the read, then return. Cost: one full round of consensus per read — far too expensive for production. Raft's optimisation, called <em>ReadIndex</em>: when a read arrives, the leader records its current commit index, sends a heartbeat to a majority of followers to confirm it is still leader, and then returns the read once its state machine has applied entries up to that index. No new log entry is written. Cost: one heartbeat round-trip. Faster alternative is the <em>leader lease</em>: the leader holds a lease (e.g. 9 ms granted by the previous heartbeat round) during which it can serve reads without any RPC. Lease reads are sub-millisecond and used by CockroachDB and TiKV for hot-path reads. Trade-off: lease reads require bounded clock skew between leader and followers (typically under 500 ms), so wall-clock synchronisation (NTP, PTP) becomes a correctness requirement, not just a hygiene practice.</p>
    <p><strong>Snapshots and log compaction.</strong> A Raft log grows forever in principle; in practice the log must be truncated, or disk fills and recovery from disk replay takes hours. Snapshots solve this: each node periodically writes its full state machine state plus the (lastIncludedIndex, lastIncludedTerm) tuple to disk, then deletes log entries up to that index. A follower that has fallen behind by more than the retention window receives an <code>InstallSnapshot</code> RPC instead of catching up via AppendEntries — the leader ships its snapshot, the follower loads it as its state, then catches up the recent log tail. Snapshot frequency is a tunable: too often hurts throughput (fsync amplification); too rare hurts recovery and grows the log. Etcd defaults to snapshotting every 10,000 entries. CockroachDB does it every few seconds. The snapshot also must include the latest cluster membership configuration — failure to include it is a famous bug source that bites operators who hand-write Raft.</p>
    <p><strong>Persistence and disk fsync.</strong> Raft mandates that every server persists three pieces of state to durable storage before responding: currentTerm, votedFor (for the current term), and the log entries (including the new ones being acknowledged in the current RPC). Skipping any one breaks safety. The fsync cost is the dominant operational expense — every committed entry costs at least one fsync on the leader and one on each follower that acknowledges. On NVMe with battery-backed cache, fsync is 50-100 microseconds; on cloud volumes (EBS gp3, GCP balanced PD), it can be 1-3 ms. This is why high-throughput Raft systems (Kafka KRaft, TiKV) batch many client writes into a single log entry and fsync to amortise the cost. The classical anti-pattern is fsync-per-request: never do it for Raft on any but trivial workloads.</p>
    <p><strong>Multi-Raft and sharding.</strong> A single Raft group has bounded throughput — proportional to the slowest replica's fsync plus the network round-trip. To scale beyond a few thousand writes per second, production systems run many independent Raft groups in parallel, each responsible for a shard of the keyspace. CockroachDB partitions its key range into "ranges" (around 512 MB each) and runs one Raft group per range. TiKV does the same with "regions." Kafka KRaft runs one Raft group per metadata topic partition. The challenge is rebalancing: when traffic shifts, ranges must split, merge, and migrate, each operation being a coordinated membership change of a Raft group. The system that orchestrates this is sometimes called the "shard manager" or "placement driver" — it itself uses Raft to make placement decisions consistent across the operator pool.</p>
    <p><strong>Leadership transfer and graceful shutdown.</strong> An operator who wants to drain a Raft node before maintenance can ask the cluster to <em>transfer leadership</em>: the current leader sends a <code>TimeoutNow</code> message to a designated follower, which immediately starts an election and is almost guaranteed to win because no other follower has timed out yet. The current leader steps down. This avoids the 150-300 ms unavailability window that a normal leader crash would cause; the user-visible impact is under 10 ms. Without leadership transfer, draining a leader for a deploy causes one election storm per leader migrated, which is fine for a 3-node cluster but punishing for a multi-Raft system with thousands of groups. CockroachDB and TiKV both expose leadership-transfer endpoints for rolling deploys.</p>
    <p><strong>Production observability for Raft.</strong> Minimum viable Raft dashboard exposes: leader uptime per group, election count per minute (a spike means flapping leadership), commit latency p50/p99 per group, log-size (uncompacted) per replica, raft_term gauge (when it climbs fast, there are repeated unsuccessful elections), heartbeat round-trip latency, follower-lag-entries (how far behind each follower is), and snapshot install rate (a high rate means followers are falling behind faster than they can catch up). Etcd ships <code>etcd_server_leader_changes_seen_total</code>, <code>etcd_disk_wal_fsync_duration_seconds</code>, and <code>etcd_network_peer_round_trip_time_seconds</code> by default. CockroachDB's liveness and range_lease_holder metrics serve the same role across thousands of Raft groups.</p>
    <p><strong>Real production failures.</strong> GitHub October 2018: a network blip between datacenters caused etcd to fail over to a partition that briefly thought it was authoritative. The actual issue was a misconfigured external sync that wrote to both regions during a brief window — Raft was correct, the orchestrator above it was not. Datadog 2023: a TiKV cluster experienced repeated leader elections under high write load because pre-vote was disabled in an older version; upgrading and enabling pre-vote fixed it. Etcd Kubernetes incident 2020: an admin removed a node from a 3-node cluster without using the proper membership change API; the remaining two nodes flipped between leader and candidate because their stored configurations disagreed. The fix: stop the cluster, hand-edit configs, restart. Lesson: never bypass joint-consensus membership change in production.</p>
    <p><strong>History: from Multi-Paxos to Raft to Multi-Raft.</strong> Leslie Lamport's Paxos (1989) was the first proven-correct consensus algorithm but notoriously obscure. Multi-Paxos optimised it for the replicated-log case but still needed careful engineering. Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout published Raft in 2014 ("In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm") with the explicit design goal of being teachable. By 2026 Raft has roughly 100 open-source implementations and is the default in every new system that needs CP consensus. Industry has converged: greenfield projects pick Raft, legacy systems sometimes retain Multi-Paxos (Spanner, Cassandra's Paxos-based LWT, Neon's safekeepers). Multi-Raft — running many Raft groups in parallel — is the design pattern that scales the technique from a single config store to a horizontally-sharded database.</p>
    <p><strong>Cross-protocol: Raft as building block.</strong> Raft is rarely the user-visible primitive. Kubernetes uses etcd's Raft to store config. Service meshes (Consul) use Raft for service discovery and KV. Databases use Raft per shard for range replication. Stream processors (Kafka KRaft, Pulsar BookKeeper) use Raft for metadata. Even some queues (NATS JetStream) use Raft for storage. The pattern is consistent: Raft replicates a small, ordered, well-defined log; the application layer on top defines what the log entries mean. Treating Raft as a primitive — replicated state machine you control by writing log entries — is the senior engineer's mental model.</p>
    <p><strong>Deployment patterns: managed vs self-hosted.</strong> Self-hosting an etcd or Consul cluster across three availability zones is the canonical "you handle the fsync latency" deployment. Cloud-managed alternatives (AWS Aurora's Raft-based storage layer, GCP Spanner, Azure Cosmos DB) absorb the operational burden but cost 2-5x more. For a typical 3-AZ deployment: place Raft nodes in three separate AZs, target intra-region latency of 1-5 ms, use NVMe-backed instances (i3, m5d), enable disk write-cache only with battery backup, and never co-locate two Raft replicas on the same host. Cross-region Raft (5 nodes split 2-2-1) is possible but pays 50-200 ms commit latency for the privilege of surviving a full region loss — Spanner and CockroachDB users routinely pay this price.</p>
