You can't trick physics. Every byte on the screen started as a perturbation in something — voltage on copper, light pulses through glass, radio waves through air. The whole stack above runs in the time it takes that perturbation to cross the link. This piece is about that lowest layer, and why the speed of light decides what you can build above it.

<Crux>What voltage, photons, and radio actually carry your packet, and why the speed of light bounds everything above.</Crux>

<TierAccordion
  id="tier-mechanism"
  lang="en"
>
  <Fragment slot="junior">
    <p>Imagine the wire is a hose. You push a bit at one end and it travels at almost the speed of light — but the hose has a fixed thickness (bandwidth), so only so many bits per second fit. Every cable, fiber, or radio link is just a fancy hose with two properties: how thick it is (bits per second) and how long it is (light-time end to end).</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p>Three modulation regimes carry virtually all the world's bits:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Differential voltage on copper (Ethernet, DSL, USB).</strong> Two wires carry mirrored signals; the receiver looks at the difference to reject common-mode noise. Gigabit Ethernet uses PAM-5 at 125 MBaud per pair, four pairs in parallel.</li>
      <li><strong>Photons through glass (single-mode and multi-mode fiber).</strong> A laser switches on and off at billions of Hz. Single-mode fiber has a 9 µm core; the light bounces less and goes farther — undersea cables run thousands of km without repeaters because erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) regenerate signals every ~80 km.</li>
      <li><strong>Modulated radio (Wi-Fi 6, 5G, satellite).</strong> Carrier frequencies in the GHz range are encoded with OFDM — splitting the spectrum into hundreds of narrow subcarriers, each carrying a few bits via QAM constellations. 1024-QAM packs 10 bits per symbol when SNR is high.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>The bit rate you get is bounded by Shannon: <code>C = B · log₂(1 + S/N)</code>. Doubling bandwidth doubles capacity; doubling SNR adds one bit per symbol. That's why 5G chases millimeter-wave (huge B) and why your phone falls back to lower QAM when it's far from the AP (low S/N).</p>
    <p>The latency floor is even harder to escape. <strong>Light travels at ~200,000 km/s in glass</strong> (slower than in vacuum). NY → London is 5,500 km, so the minimum theoretical round-trip is 55 ms, achieved by no real cable. Actual round-trip 70–90 ms. NY → Sydney is ~16,000 km, RTT ~210 ms. <strong>You cannot make a request to Sydney appear instant. You can only design around it.</strong></p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p>Several second-order effects to know:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Bufferbloat at the physical edge.</strong> DOCSIS modems and LTE basebands carry 100–500 ms of buffer; a saturated link queues packets longer than the actual transit. CAKE and fq_codel on the customer-side router cap this.</li>
      <li><strong>BBR vs CUBIC over satellite.</strong> LEO links (Starlink ~25 ms one-way) behave like terrestrial. GEO (~250 ms round-trip) starves loss-based congestion control entirely; BBR's RTT probing recovers most of the throughput.</li>
      <li><strong>PoE budgets and 802.3bt.</strong> A 90 W class-8 port is enough for a small switch, but cable resistance over 100 m of Cat6 eats ~15 W — the budget on the device side is closer to 71 W.</li>
      <li><strong>Coding gain.</strong> 10G Ethernet over copper uses LDPC with Reed-Solomon outer; the BER target is 10⁻¹² post-FEC. The raw channel is 10⁻⁶; almost all the difference is FEC.</li>
    </ul>
  </Fragment>
</TierAccordion>

<Misconception id="r-failure" title="Heads-up: bandwidth is not latency">
You cannot solve latency by buying bandwidth. A 10 Gb/s link doesn't move one byte faster than 100 Mb/s — bytes travel at light speed. More bandwidth increases throughput, not speed. Pre-position data (CDN) or eliminate round-trips (HTTP/2 push, QUIC 0-RTT, TLS resumption).
</Misconception>

The physical layer also defines the ceiling for many higher-layer choices. TCP slow start opens the congestion window one RTT at a time. With 80 ms RTT, getting a window of 64 MTUs takes about 6 round trips — half a second of warmup before you reach line rate. This is why <SpiralCue thread="latency" lang="en" /> is the thread you'll see thread through every chapter that follows. The physical layer sets the budget; everything above is allocating it.
