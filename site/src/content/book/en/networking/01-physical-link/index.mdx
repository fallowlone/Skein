You can't trick physics. Every byte on the screen started as a perturbation in something — voltage on copper, light pulses through glass, radio waves through air. The whole stack above runs in the time it takes that perturbation to cross the link. This piece is about that lowest layer, and why the speed of light decides what you can build above it.

<Crux>What voltage, photons, and radio actually carry your packet, and why the speed of light bounds everything above.</Crux>

    <p><strong>What this layer does in one sentence.</strong> It carries actual bits — voltage on copper, light through glass fibre, radio waves through air — from one physical device to another. Everything else in the network stack rides on top of these bits arriving safely.</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Every higher protocol (IP, TCP, TLS, HTTP) has to wait for the physical layer to move the bits. The speed of light through fibre is finite — you cannot make a request to Sydney appear instant. Knowing the floor numbers means understanding which optimisations are actually possible.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> Imagine the wire is a hose. You push a bit at one end and it travels at almost the speed of light — but the hose has a fixed thickness (bandwidth), so only so many bits per second fit. Every cable, fibre, or radio link is just a fancy hose with two properties: how thick it is (bits per second) and how long it is (light-time end to end). Two locations 5,500 km apart have an unavoidable round-trip of at least ~55 ms regardless of bandwidth.</p>
    <p><strong>Read-aloud dialogue.</strong> Your computer to its network card: "I want to send this packet." Network card to the cable: "Encode these bits as voltage changes." Cable to the next switch: "Voltage arrives at the other end, decode back to bits." Same logic for fibre (light pulses) and Wi-Fi (radio waves). All physical media do the same job: turn bits into a signal that travels, then back into bits.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario end to end.</strong> You click a link. Browser builds a request. Operating system hands packet bytes to the network driver. Network card encodes bytes as voltage / light / radio. The signal travels — copper (your home), fibre (long distance), radio (wifi, cellular). At each hop the receiver decodes back to bytes, the router processes the packet (next layer: <a href="/en/networking/02-ip-packet/">IP packet</a>), re-encodes for the next hop. Repeat 10-15 times until the bits arrive at the destination's network card.</p>

    <p>Three modulation regimes carry virtually all the world's bits:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Differential voltage on copper (Ethernet, DSL, USB).</strong> Two wires carry mirrored signals; the receiver looks at the difference to reject common-mode noise. Gigabit Ethernet uses PAM-5 at 125 MBaud per pair, four pairs in parallel.</li>
      <li><strong>Photons through glass (single-mode and multi-mode fiber).</strong> A laser switches on and off at billions of Hz. Single-mode fiber has a 9 µm core; the light bounces less and goes farther — undersea cables run thousands of km without repeaters because erbium-doped fiber amplifiers (EDFAs) regenerate signals every ~80 km.</li>
      <li><strong>Modulated radio (Wi-Fi 6, 5G, satellite).</strong> Carrier frequencies in the GHz range are encoded with OFDM — splitting the spectrum into hundreds of narrow subcarriers, each carrying a few bits via QAM constellations. 1024-QAM packs 10 bits per symbol when SNR is high.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>The bit rate you get is bounded by Shannon: <code>C = B · log₂(1 + S/N)</code>. Doubling bandwidth doubles capacity; doubling SNR adds one bit per symbol. That's why 5G chases millimeter-wave (huge B) and why your phone falls back to lower QAM when it's far from the AP (low S/N).</p>
    <p>The latency floor is even harder to escape. <strong>Light travels at ~200,000 km/s in glass</strong> (slower than in vacuum). NY → London is 5,500 km, so the minimum theoretical round-trip is 55 ms, achieved by no real cable. Actual round-trip 70–90 ms. NY → Sydney is ~16,000 km, RTT ~210 ms. <strong>You cannot make a request to Sydney appear instant. You can only design around it.</strong></p>
    <p><strong>Ethernet at the link layer.</strong> Most desk and datacentre Ethernet today runs at 1 Gbps (1000BASE-T over Cat5e/Cat6 copper) or 10/25/100 Gbps (over fibre or 10GBASE-T copper). Frames have a 14-byte header (destination MAC, source MAC, EtherType) and a 4-byte FCS (frame check sequence, CRC-32). Maximum frame size is 1518 bytes (MTU 1500 + headers) or 9018 bytes for jumbo frames. Inside one collision domain, switches forward by destination MAC after MAC-table lookup; they "learn" MAC-to-port mappings by observing source MACs of frames they see. ARP (covered in 02-ip-packet) resolves IP-to-MAC for nodes in the same L2 segment.</p>
    <p><strong>Wi-Fi essentials.</strong> 802.11 standards: a/b/g (legacy) up to Wi-Fi 6E (802.11ax) and Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be, standardised Jan 2024). Channels in 2.4 GHz (20 MHz wide, 14 channels worldwide but only 3 non-overlapping), 5 GHz (20/40/80/160 MHz, 25+ channels), and 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E, 60+ channels). MIMO (multiple antennas) and MU-MIMO multiply throughput by spatial multiplexing. Beamforming directs signal energy toward the client. Real-world throughput rarely matches "marketing" rate — 802.11ax claims 9.6 Gbps theoretical, typical user gets 300-800 Mbps at 5 m. Wi-Fi 7 introduces 320 MHz channels and 4096-QAM encoding, raising theoretical peak to 46 Gbps; practical deployment starts 2025–2026 in flagship devices, with Multi-Link Operation (MLO) enabling simultaneous dual-band streams reaching 3–5 Gbps per client in clean RF environments.</p>
    <p><strong>Cellular generations and latency.</strong> 4G LTE typical RTT 30-60 ms, throughput 10-100 Mbps. 5G NR (sub-6 GHz) typical RTT 15-30 ms, throughput 100 Mbps - 1 Gbps. 5G mmWave delivers gigabit speeds at sub-10 ms RTT but only over very short ranges (~200 m line-of-sight). Cellular latency is dominated by base-station scheduling and uplink-access timing, not propagation. Modern 5G aims for sub-1 ms air-interface latency for industrial applications via URLLC (Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication).</p>
    <p><strong>Cable + DSL + FTTH for residential.</strong> DOCSIS (cable modems): typical 200-500 Mbps down, 20-50 Mbps up. Latency 10-30 ms. Often suffers bufferbloat under load. DSL: 5-100 Mbps over twisted-pair phone lines. Fibre to the home (FTTH/GPON, XGS-PON): 1 Gbps symmetric typical, 10 Gbps available in newer deployments. Latency low (sub-5 ms to ISP edge). The big trend: fibre has displaced copper everywhere it's economically viable; remote areas still depend on cable, DSL, or satellite.</p>
    <p><strong>Switches vs hubs vs routers.</strong> A hub (now obsolete) repeats every signal on every port — a single collision domain. A switch (modern Ethernet) forwards based on MAC table — each port is its own collision domain, full-duplex. A router (IP layer) forwards based on routing table at L3. Most "switches" sold today are managed L2/L3 hybrids capable of basic IP routing. Datacentre fabric is typically Clos-topology spine-leaf switches with L3 between leaves for ECMP load-balancing.</p>
    <p><strong>Power over Ethernet (PoE).</strong> Standardised by 802.3af (15.4 W), 802.3at (PoE+, 30 W), and 802.3bt (PoE++, 60-90 W per port at class 8). Powers small devices (Wi-Fi APs, IP phones, cameras, IoT) over the same Ethernet cable that carries data. Power is injected by the switch and delivered over unused or signal pairs depending on type. Cable resistance over 100 m of Cat6 dissipates a meaningful fraction of the budget — 90 W at the switch becomes ~71 W at the device side. Knowing your PoE budget is essential when planning IoT or AP deployments.</p>
    <p><strong>Optical fibre: single-mode vs multi-mode.</strong> Multi-mode fibre (MMF) has a thick core (50 or 62.5 µm); the light bounces around inside the core, supporting many "modes." Cheap LEDs work as the source. Distance limit ~500 m at 1 Gbps, less at higher rates. Used inside datacentres and buildings. Single-mode fibre (SMF) has a 9 µm core; only one mode propagates. Requires expensive laser sources but supports much longer distances — 10 km, 40 km, 80 km, or with EDFA amplifiers thousands of kilometres. All submarine cables are SMF with EDFAs every ~80 km regenerating the signal.</p>
    <p><strong>Signal degradation and coding gain.</strong> Real channels have noise, attenuation, and dispersion. Forward Error Correction (FEC) adds redundant bits so the receiver can correct errors without retransmission. 10G Ethernet over copper uses Low-Density Parity Check (LDPC) inner + Reed-Solomon outer; raw bit-error rate (BER) is ~10⁻⁶ but post-FEC BER hits 10⁻¹². Submarine cables use stronger FEC (LDPC + concatenated codes); the coding gain is the difference in SNR required at the same BER. Modern systems can squeeze ~10 bits per symbol out of dense modulation (1024-QAM or higher) when SNR allows.</p>
    <p><strong>Bandwidth-delay product (BDP).</strong> BDP = bandwidth × RTT. On a 10 Gbps link with 100 ms RTT, BDP = 10 Gbps × 0.1 s = 1 Gbit = 125 MB. To fully use the link, your TCP receive window must be that big or larger. Default Linux receive window scaling allows up to 4 MB; modern stacks auto-tune. On high-BDP paths (satellite, intercontinental fibre) TCP underutilises without correct window sizing. BBR congestion control models BDP explicitly and paces sends to match.</p>
    <p><strong>Misconception block.</strong> Three things commonly confused. One: "Higher bandwidth fixes latency." False — bandwidth is throughput capacity; latency is round-trip time. Doubling bandwidth doesn't reduce ping. Two: "Wi-Fi is the same as Ethernet for performance." False — Wi-Fi has shared-medium effects (collisions, contention), higher and more variable latency, and lower effective throughput than wired. Three: "5G replaces fibre." False — 5G last-mile is convenient but the backhaul is fibre. Fibre to the cellular base station carries the traffic.</p>
    <p><strong>The Internet's physical backbone.</strong> ~500+ submarine cables connect the continents. Cable owners (consortiums of Tier-1 ISPs, Google, Meta, Microsoft) operate them. Each cable carries 10-30 Tbps via dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM) — many different wavelengths of light on the same fibre, each carrying ~100 Gbps. Cable failures happen monthly (anchor strikes, undersea landslides, deliberate sabotage), but redundancy and rerouting keep traffic flowing. The submarinecablemap.com site visualises the global mesh — striking how much the physical Internet is shaped by ocean geography.</p>
    <p><strong>Local-area network basics.</strong> Inside a building or campus, Ethernet dominates. Twisted-pair copper (Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6a) for short runs to user devices; multi-mode fibre between switches in long buildings. The LAN has its own MAC-layer addressing (48-bit hardware addresses) and uses ARP to map IP addresses to MACs in the same broadcast domain. VLANs (802.1Q) partition one physical LAN into multiple logical networks via 12-bit VLAN tags in frame headers. Spanning Tree Protocol (STP, 802.1D) prevents loops in switched networks by computing a tree of preferred paths and blocking redundant links — newer variants RSTP and MSTP converge faster.</p>
    <p><strong>NICs and offload.</strong> Server-grade Network Interface Cards include hardware offload features: TCP/UDP checksum offload, TCP segmentation offload (TSO — chop large send buffer into MSS-sized packets in the NIC), Large Receive Offload (LRO — coalesce received segments before delivery to kernel), generic receive offload (GRO). Modern NICs add RoCE (RDMA over Ethernet), VXLAN/Geneve overlay encap/decap, TLS record encryption (kTLS hardware), eBPF/XDP programmable packet processing. The CPU does less work per packet; throughput scales without proportional CPU consumption. Important for ML/AI clusters where each GPU needs full-line-rate connectivity to others.</p>
    <p><strong>Cable types and use cases.</strong> Cat5e: up to 1 Gbps, 100 m. Cat6: 1 Gbps reliably + 10 Gbps up to 55 m. Cat6a: 10 Gbps up to 100 m. Cat7/Cat8: higher-rated but rarely used in practice (fibre cheaper at that speed). Coaxial: legacy cable TV/Internet (DOCSIS); still common at last-mile. Twinax (DAC, direct attach copper): short fibre alternative for in-rack server-to-switch up to ~10 m; cheap and low-latency. Active Optical Cables (AOC): same form factor as DAC but with embedded optics for longer runs. Choosing the right cable is mostly an exercise in matching length, rate, and EMI environment to budget.</p>
    <p><strong>Standards bodies and where specs come from.</strong> IEEE 802.x defines Ethernet (802.3), Wi-Fi (802.11), Bluetooth (802.15.1, now Bluetooth SIG), VLANs (802.1Q). IETF defines IP and above. 3GPP defines cellular (4G, 5G, 6G). ITU-T defines optical transport (G.709, G.992). USB-IF defines USB. The standards process is slow but the resulting interoperability is what makes the Internet a single connected system across thousands of vendors. Knowing which body owns which layer helps when reading specs and tracking emerging features.</p>
    <p><strong>Practical debugging at the link layer.</strong> Tools you'll reach for repeatedly: `ip -s link show <iface>` reveals interface statistics including drops, errors, and overruns. `ethtool` queries and configures NIC settings — speed, duplex, auto-negotiation. `ethtool -S <iface>` dumps hundreds of vendor counters. `mii-tool` (older) and `dmesg` show link-state changes. For Wi-Fi: `iwconfig`, `iw dev <iface> link`, `iw dev <iface> scan`. For physical-layer diagnosis you sometimes need a hardware tool — a cable certifier verifies Cat6 wiring meets spec, an optical power meter measures fibre signal strength, a Wi-Fi analyser tool visualises spectrum usage. Without the right tool the symptoms look mysterious; with it the answer is usually obvious in minutes.</p>

  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p><strong>Bufferbloat at the physical edge.</strong> DOCSIS modems and LTE basebands carry 100–500 ms of buffer; a saturated link queues packets longer than the actual transit. The result: ping jumps from 20 ms to 500 ms under load, video calls stutter, interactive apps feel broken even though throughput numbers look fine. Cause: vendors over-buffered to avoid drops, which TCP needs for congestion signalling. Fix: smart queue management (SQM) at the bottleneck — CAKE, fq_codel, or PIE — drops packets earlier and fairly across flows, capping queueing delay even under saturation. Modern home routers (OpenWrt, Eero, modern carrier-provided) enable SQM by default; older ones don't and bufferbloat persists invisibly.</p>
    <p><strong>BBR vs CUBIC over satellite.</strong> LEO links (Starlink ~25 ms one-way to satellite, ~50-60 ms total RTT) behave like terrestrial; standard CUBIC works fine. GEO (~250 ms one-way, ~600 ms RTT) starves loss-based congestion control entirely — by the time a loss signal reaches the sender, multiple windows have been transmitted. BBR's RTT probing recovers most of the throughput because it doesn't depend on loss signalling. Cellular operators deploy CAKE-style SQM at radio cells to control bufferbloat across mobile users sharing the air interface. The pattern: BBR + AQM + small buffers wins on high-latency lossy paths; CUBIC + larger buffers wins on terrestrial wired.</p>
    <p><strong>PoE budgets and 802.3bt.</strong> A 90 W class-8 port is enough for a small switch, but cable resistance over 100 m of Cat6 eats ~15 W — the budget on the device side is closer to 71 W. PoE++ (802.3bt) defines four pairs at 60 W (Type 3) or 90 W (Type 4); requires Cat6 or better cable. Devices that exceed budget brown out under load; switch firmware must arbitrate among ports if total PoE draw exceeds the supply's headroom. Worth knowing for any IoT or Wi-Fi AP deployment — PoE planning prevents intermittent failures that look like network problems but are actually power.</p>
    <p><strong>Coding gain and FEC details.</strong> 10G Ethernet over copper uses Low-Density Parity Check (LDPC) inner code with Reed-Solomon outer; the BER target is 10⁻¹² post-FEC. The raw channel is 10⁻⁶; almost all the difference is FEC. Submarine optical systems use concatenated codes with iterative decoding — coding gains of 10+ dB are typical, letting the same optical SNR carry far more bits per symbol. Modern 100G and 400G Ethernet over fibre use 256-QAM PAM-4 with strong FEC; the SNR margin is small and any cable degradation shows up as sudden BER cliff rather than gradual degradation.</p>
    <p><strong>Optical fibre dispersion and impairments.</strong> Long-distance single-mode fibre suffers chromatic dispersion (different wavelengths travel at different speeds), polarisation-mode dispersion (orthogonal polarisations travel at slightly different speeds), and four-wave mixing (nonlinear effects between adjacent DWDM channels). Dispersion compensation modules (DCMs) and modern coherent optical transceivers actively compensate. Coherent detection (used in 100G+ systems) recovers phase information as well as amplitude, enabling higher-order modulation. The result: modern long-haul fibre carries 800 Gbps per wavelength with 60+ wavelengths per fibre = 48 Tbps per fibre pair.</p>
    <p><strong>Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) and what's new.</strong> Channel widths up to 320 MHz (vs 160 MHz max in Wi-Fi 6). 4096-QAM (vs 1024-QAM) packs 12 bits per symbol instead of 10. Multi-Link Operation (MLO) lets one client use multiple bands simultaneously — sending different streams on 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz concurrently. Wireless Broadband Alliance field trials (March 2026) with AT&T and Intel show 75% downlink + 116% uplink throughput improvement under co-channel interference; real gigabit-per-stream performance in good conditions. Caveat: requires Wi-Fi 7 client + AP + 6 GHz availability + clean RF environment. Wi-Fi 6E (6 GHz extension) remains dominant in 2024-2026; Wi-Fi 7 just emerging in flagship devices.</p>
    <p><strong>5G and 6G frontiers.</strong> 5G has three pillars: enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB, fast normal traffic), Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC, sub-1 ms air interface for industrial robotics), Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC, supporting 1M+ IoT devices per square km). Network slicing partitions the radio + core resources to deliver each. 5G Standalone (SA) cores are still being deployed; many 5G deployments are Non-Standalone (NSA) riding on 4G core. 5G-Advanced (3GPP Release 18) was finalised mid-2024 with commercial deployments rolling out late 2025 onward; major carriers (T-Mobile, SK Telecom, China Mobile) are activating Release 18 features including AI/ML air-interface optimisation, 20%+ RAN power reduction, and expanded satellite integration (NTN). 6G research (2030+) explores terahertz frequencies, AI-native air interface, integrated sensing and communication. The deployment timeline is measured in years — even 5G continues rolling out a decade after standardisation.</p>
    <p><strong>Datacentre fabric specifics.</strong> Modern hyperscale datacentres run Clos-topology fabrics with spine-leaf switching. Each leaf switch (rack-top) has multiple uplinks to spine switches; ECMP across uplinks gives non-blocking bisection bandwidth within the fabric. Cables: 100/400 Gbps optical between spine and leaf, 25/100 Gbps from servers to leaf. RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE) bypasses kernel network stack for direct memory access between machines — critical for GPU clusters running distributed training. Lossless Ethernet (priority-based flow control, ECN) is required for RoCE to not drop and crash flows.</p>
    <p><strong>Cable plant operations.</strong> Real datacentres burn surprising hours on cabling. Bend radius limits on fibre, twist-pair routing inside racks, labelling discipline, redundant paths between rows. Bad cabling causes silent intermittent problems — flapping links, increased BER, even mysterious server reboots if PoE budgets are mis-planned. Vendor-neutral colour codes (yellow for single-mode, orange for OM2 multi-mode, aqua for OM3, magenta for OM4) help avoid mis-matched optics. Operations teams treat the cable plant as core infrastructure with change-control discipline.</p>
    <p><strong>800G and beyond: 2026 datacenter inflection.</strong> 800 Gigabit Ethernet (IEEE 802.3df, approved Feb 2024) is now shipping in production; vendors (Arista 7800R3/R4, Broadcom Tomahawk 6, Cisco Silicon One G300) deliver 100+ Tbps aggregate fabric capacity. 2026 projection: 800G port shipments exceed 60% of high-end datacenter volumes, driven by AI/ML cluster demand. Hyperscalers are ordering 800G transceivers at scale — Q2 2026 orders exceed $53M. The next frontier is 1.6T Ethernet: IEEE 802.3dj standard completion expected July 2026, with early 2026–2027 deployments anticipated. These rates push both optical design (dense DWDM, coherent detection) and power limits (800G optics consume notable power; cooling becomes architecture-critical). Datacentre fabric design is shifting from 400G as baseline to 800G as default spine-leaf uplink, with 1.6T reserved for ultra-high-end training clusters.</p>
    <p><strong>Hardware offload and the kernel-bypass trend.</strong> Modern NICs (SmartNICs, DPUs from Nvidia/AMD/Pensando) implement TCP segmentation offload (TSO), large receive offload (LRO), TLS offload (kTLS), RoCE, DPDK kernel bypass, eBPF/XDP for programmable packet processing. The data plane increasingly lives in the NIC; the host CPU just initialises and monitors. AWS Nitro is a public example — VM networking entirely offloaded to the Nitro card. Linux's io_uring + AF_XDP enable userspace zero-copy paths to the NIC for any application willing to bypass standard sockets.</p>
    <p><strong>Submarine cable economics and politics.</strong> A new transatlantic cable costs roughly $300 million; lifetime 25+ years. Cable consortiums (e.g. MAREA shared by Microsoft, Facebook, Telxius) split costs. Geopolitical concerns: cable landing points are critical infrastructure. The 2024 Baltic Sea cable incident (multiple cables severed in suspicious circumstances) led NATO to launch dedicated cable-monitoring missions. Submarine cable repair ships are limited in number worldwide; major outages can take weeks to fix. New tech: hollow-core fibre (light travels in air instead of glass, ~50% faster) — currently in field trials, could reshape latency budgets if deployed at scale.</p>
    <p><strong>Power and cooling — the other physical limits.</strong> Datacentre power consumption is now significant fraction of national grids (~3% globally, growing fast with AI). PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) ratios above 1.5 are wasteful; hyperscalers chase 1.1-1.2 via free-air cooling, evaporative cooling, immersion cooling. Liquid cooling becomes mandatory for ML/HPC racks (50+ kW per rack vs traditional 5-15 kW). Stranded power capacity in older datacentres limits deployment of modern GPU clusters even when network and compute are available. The physical layer of the Internet is increasingly bottlenecked by megawatts, not megabits.</p>
    <p><strong>Latency math you should memorise.</strong> Light in vacuum 300,000 km/s; in glass ~200,000 km/s. Per 100 km of fibre: ~0.5 ms one-way. Same continent (1,000-3,000 km): 5-15 ms one-way, 10-30 ms RTT. Transatlantic (~5,500 km): 28 ms one-way, 60-90 ms RTT (real cable adds ~30% over theoretical minimum). Transpacific (~16,000 km): 80 ms one-way, 180-220 ms RTT. LEO satellite (~550 km altitude): 4-15 ms one-way to satellite, ~20-50 ms RTT. GEO satellite (~36,000 km altitude): 120 ms one-way to satellite, ~600 ms RTT. These are floors — actual measurements include router queueing, last-mile, and routing inefficiency on top.</p>
    <p><strong>The unified mental model.</strong> Every byte you've ever sent or received traversed a physical medium — copper, fibre, or air — at some fraction of light speed. The medium has a bandwidth (bits per second) and a propagation delay (length divided by signal speed). Higher protocols layer on top but cannot beat these physics. Optimisation work means recognising which layer dominates each request. Bandwidth-bound: upgrade the link, increase parallelism, compress. Latency-bound: move content closer (CDN), eliminate round-trips (cache, 0-RTT), parallelise within round-trips. Knowing the physical layer means knowing the floor you cannot escape.</p>
    <p><strong>What to watch for the rest of this decade.</strong> Hollow-core fibre (light through air in a microscopic glass structure) ~50% faster than solid glass — first field trials in 2023, commercial deployment expected late-2020s. Co-packaged optics (CPO) integrate fibre transceivers directly into switch ASICs, cutting per-port power and enabling higher fabric densities. LEO satellite constellations (Starlink, Project Kuiper, OneWeb) reach 200,000+ active satellites, reshaping the latency vs coverage trade-off for rural and mobile users. 800G Ethernet (already shipping) and 1.6T Ethernet (2026 standard) push backbone capacity. The interesting tension: AI workloads demanding ever more bandwidth per rack while submarine-cable capacity grows linearly — expect interesting infrastructure investments through the decade.</p>
    <p><strong>Quantum networking on the horizon.</strong> Quantum key distribution (QKD) over fibre enables unconditionally-secure key exchange — currently in pilot deployments between government and bank facilities in select Chinese and European cities. Range limit ~100 km without quantum repeaters; quantum repeaters remain a research challenge. Less hyped but more practical: post-quantum cryptography (ML-KEM, ML-DSA — covered in 05-tls-handshake) protects against future quantum computers breaking classical RSA/ECDH. Quantum networking is decades away from changing day-to-day networking; PQ cryptography is here now and shaping TLS evolution this decade.</p>
    <p><strong>Reading the spec for any link technology.</strong> When you encounter an unfamiliar link technology — say a new Wi-Fi version, a new optical standard, an industrial fieldbus — three pieces of data tell you 80% of what you need: physical medium (copper / fibre / radio frequency), encoding scheme (modulation + line coding + FEC), and frame format (header + payload boundaries). With these three you can estimate bandwidth, latency, error characteristics, and where deployments will struggle. Specs are long; cross-referencing those three sections in any IEEE/ITU document gets you operational understanding faster than reading cover-to-cover.</p>

<Misconception id="r-failure" title="Heads-up: bandwidth is not latency">
You cannot solve latency by buying bandwidth. A 10 Gb/s link doesn't move one byte faster than 100 Mb/s — bytes travel at light speed. More bandwidth increases throughput, not speed. Pre-position data (CDN) or eliminate round-trips (HTTP/2 push, QUIC 0-RTT, TLS resumption).
</Misconception>

The physical layer also defines the ceiling for many higher-layer choices. TCP slow start opens the congestion window one RTT at a time. With 80 ms RTT, getting a window of 64 MTUs takes about 6 round trips — half a second of warmup before you reach line rate. This is why <SpiralCue thread="latency" lang="en" /> is the thread you'll see thread through every chapter that follows. The physical layer sets the budget; everything above is allocating it.

<KeyTakeaway>
Every byte of Internet traffic travels as voltage on copper, light pulses through glass fibre, or modulated radio waves through air. The medium has a bandwidth (bits per second, determined by Shannon's theorem) and a propagation delay (length divided by signal speed, ~200,000 km/s in fibre). Higher protocols layer on top but cannot beat these physics — a New-York-to-Sydney request will always take at least ~200 ms round-trip. Practical engineering means knowing the floor numbers, recognising bandwidth-bound vs latency-bound problems, and applying the right fix: more parallelism / wider links / compression for bandwidth, or CDN edges / cache hits / round-trip elimination for latency.
</KeyTakeaway>
