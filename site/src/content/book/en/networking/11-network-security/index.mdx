Your service is working fine. Then, without warning, requests start timing out. The traffic looks normal on your monitor — same request count, same sizes — but responses slow from 50 ms to 5 seconds. You didn't deploy anything. You check the logs and see 10 million requests per second from a hundred thousand different IPs. An adversary is attacking. Network security is the discipline of detecting and stopping that attack while keeping your legitimate users fast.

<Crux>How DDoS attacks work and how to defend with rate limiting, firewalls, WAFs, and anycast scrubbing centers.</Crux>

The attacks come in three flavors. Volumetric attacks (L3/L4) aim to saturate the network pipe itself. State-exhaustion attacks aim to fill connection-tracking tables and exhaust memory. Application-layer attacks (L7) look like legitimate traffic and are the hardest to stop. <SpiralCue thread="statefulness" lang="en" /> — network security is about defending the state your infrastructure builds in response to traffic. <SpiralCue thread="latency" lang="en" /> — the cost of defense (rate limiting, inspection, firewalls) must not sacrifice your legitimate users' latency.

    <p><strong>What DDoS means.</strong> Distributed Denial of Service — an attacker sends so much traffic from so many different sources that your service cannot respond to real users. The attacker does not steal data or inject commands. They just make your service unavailable.</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> DDoS attacks happen constantly. Cloudflare blocks on average ~110 million DDoS requests per day. A good DDoS defense keeps your service online when you are under attack; a bad one means downtime or expensive overprovisioning.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> Imagine a small coffee shop with one counter and one barista. A malicious customer brings a hundred friends to order simultaneously. The barista cannot serve all of them in time. That is volumetric attack. Now imagine a group of people ordering, then walking out without paying — the barista loses money but the shop stays open. That is a different attack: fraud, not denial of service. DDoS is pure bottleneck saturation.</p>
    <p><strong>Read-aloud dialogue.</strong> <PersonaTag id="bea" lang="en" /> runs a web service. <PersonaTag id="sven" lang="en" /> says "I'll flood you with so much traffic you cannot serve real users." Bea replies "I have a rate limiter — I reject the thousandth request per second from any IP." Sven says "I will use a thousand different IPs." Bea: "Then I have a WAF to detect the attack pattern. Legitimate users make normal requests; your flood is suspicious." This is the eternal arms race: attackers get more clever, defenses get smarter.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario end to end.</strong> You just launched a new startup. A competitor or a hacker wants to take you down. They use a botnet (malware on thousands of computers and IoT devices) to send HTTP requests to your origin. <a href="/en/networking/03-tcp-handshake/">TCP handshakes</a> fail because the server is overwhelmed. Legitimate users see "connection timeout." Your origin server logs fill with billions of bytes. If you have no DDoS defense, you are down. If you use a CDN with built-in DDoS scrubbing (Cloudflare, Akamai), the traffic is absorbed at the edge and only clean traffic reaches your origin. You stay online.</p>
    <p><strong>Three types of attacks.</strong> Volumetric attacks flood the network pipe (gigabits per second). Protocol attacks exhaust device memory or CPU. Application-layer attacks look like real traffic but are expensive to process. Defending all three requires different tools: for volumetric, raw bandwidth at the edge (a CDN with scrubbing centers); for protocol, connection-state rate limiting; for L7, a WAF that understands HTTP.</p>

    <p><strong>The three DDoS classes.</strong> Volumetric attacks (L3/L4) aim to saturate bandwidth: UDP floods, DNS amplification, ICMP floods. A single packet is small, but millions of them consume gigabits per second. The attacker's goal is to exhaust the pipe before hitting the server. Protocol or state-exhaustion attacks (L3/L4) aim to fill connection-tracking tables: SYN floods (thousands of half-open TCP connections trigger the server to drop new legitimate connections), ACK floods. Application-layer attacks (L7) aim to consume CPU and memory on the origin with requests that look legitimate but are expensive to process: HTTP floods, Slowloris (keep a thousand connections open, send one byte per second to hold resources indefinitely), expensive-query floods on databases. L7 attacks are the hardest to defend because legitimate traffic looks identical until you inspect the content or measure the cost.</p>
    <p><strong>Amplification and reflection.</strong> An amplification attack exploits a vulnerable third-party server: the attacker sends a small spoofed request to the third-party with a fake return address (the victim's IP), the third-party responds with a much larger answer, and the victim is hit by the response. The "amplification factor" is the ratio of response size to request size. DNS: 40-byte query → ~1700-byte response = 42x amplification. NTP: 40-byte query → 468-byte response = 12x, but some NTP implementations return up to 556x. Memcached (UDP mode, usually disabled but still exposed): 50-byte request → 51,200-byte response = 1024x amplification. Attacks using memcached or misconfigured DNS open-resolvers can generate 100+ Gbps from a handful of attacker machines. Billion-scale botnets can exceed record sizes: in December 2025 a botnet of 1-4 million Android TV devices launched a 31.4 Tbps attack on Aisuru, the largest on record. HTTP/2 Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487, CVSS 7.5) exploits HTTP/2 stream cancellation to send and instantly reset thousands of requests per second; Google absorbed 398 million requests per second from just 20,000 botnet machines.</p>
    <p><strong>Rate limiting algorithms.</strong> Token bucket: tokens accumulate in a bucket at a fixed rate (e.g., one token per millisecond) up to a maximum capacity. Each request costs one token; if the bucket is empty the request is rejected. Allows controlled bursts (if the bucket is full at rest, the first batch of requests drains it then rate limits). Leaky bucket: requests queue and drain at a fixed rate, smoothing traffic but adding latency. Fixed window: count requests in a time window (e.g., per second); simple but has a boundary-burst problem (two requests at the 59-second mark and two at the 01-second mark looks like four per second but hits twice the per-second limit in a single interval). Sliding window log: store every request timestamp; reject if more than N requests within the last T seconds; most accurate but high memory overhead. Sliding window counter: blend fixed-window and log; scale the previous window's count by the overlap with the current window, add current window's count; good practical choice for distributed systems. Token bucket is the most common default for public APIs because it is simple and allows legitimate burst traffic.</p>
    <p><strong>Where to enforce rate limits.</strong> At the edge (CDN, DDoS scrubber): blocks the largest attacks before they enter your network. At the gateway/load balancer: can rate-limit per user, per API key, or per origin connection. At the application: can rate-limit per user, per endpoint, or per business-logic metric (e.g., max three failed login attempts per minute). Distributed rate limiting (shared state across multiple nodes via Redis) adds latency but ensures a user cannot bypass limits by hitting different servers. Enforce at all three layers: edge stops volumetric floods, gateway stops bot-like patterns, app-layer stops abuse by authenticated users.</p>
    <p><strong>Adaptive/concurrency limiting.</strong> Instead of a per-second count, limit the number of in-flight requests. When concurrency peaks (queue grows), new requests are rejected or shed (dropped) to protect the service. Load shedding is brutal but effective: it prevents cascading failure (if one backend slows, rejecting new requests to that backend keeps others responsive). Implement by tracking queue depth and rejecting requests once depth exceeds a threshold. The tradeoff: users see errors instead of waiting, but the service remains responsive for the rest of your users.</p>
    <p><strong>mTLS for service-to-service identity.</strong> Mutual TLS: both client and server present X.509 certificates to each other, not just the server to the client. Proves the service's identity bidirectionally. In a service mesh (Istio, Linkerd) every sidecar proxy uses mTLS to talk to peer sidecars, enforcing zero-trust: a request from Pod A to Pod B is encrypted and authenticated. SPIFFE (Secure Production Identity Framework for Everyone) issues short-lived certificates (often ~1-hour expiration) via an SDS (Service Directory Service) that the sidecar loads and rotates automatically. The sidecar does not leak the cert key because the key never leaves the local container; the control plane refreshes the cert out-of-band. Cost: every handshake takes CPU (20-50 ms per new connection on older hardware) and cert rotation adds operational complexity. Benefit: you prevent lateral movement if the container network is compromised, and you get cryptographic proof that every service talking to you is one you authorized.</p>
    <p><strong>TLS stripping and HSTS.</strong> SSL stripping (Moxie Marlinspike, 2009): a MITM keeps the user on HTTP while talking HTTPS to the real server, decrypting both directions. The user never knows they are on plaintext. Defense: HTTP Strict Transport Security (HSTS) — a header `Strict-Transport-Security: max-age=31536000` tells the browser "only use HTTPS for this origin for the next year." But the header is sent over the response; if the first request is stripped, the header is stripped too. Solution: HSTS preload (a browser-shipped list of domains that must always be HTTPS). Google, Firefox, Chrome, and Safari ship preload lists; you request inclusion for your domain, it gets shipped in the next browser release, and HTTPS is enforced before your domain is ever visited. Min max-age for preload is 31,536,000 seconds (1 year).</p>
    <p><strong>Firewalls vs WAFs.</strong> A firewall (L3/L4) inspects IP and TCP headers: source IP, destination port, TCP flags. It cannot see encrypted HTTPS payloads. It blocks port scanning and obviously-wrong traffic. A Web Application Firewall (WAF, L7) inspects HTTP request/response content: examines headers, query parameters, body content, matches against known-attack patterns (OWASP Top 10: SQLi, XSS, path traversal, etc.). A WAF needs plaintext to inspect, so it decrypts TLS at the edge or on the origin. Both are necessary: the firewall stops network-level nonsense, the WAF stops application-level attacks. Neither is sufficient alone.</p>
    <p><strong>WAF detection modes.</strong> Signature-based: match against a database of known attacks (e.g., OWASP ModSecurity Core Rule Set contains patterns like `union select`, `<script>`, `../../../etc/passwd`). Fast, low false positives, but misses zero-days and evaded patterns. Anomaly/ML-based: model "normal" traffic, flag outliers as suspicious. Catches novel attacks, but requires tuning; too strict and legitimate users get blocked, too loose and attacks slip through. The OWASP CRS uses anomaly scoring: each rule assigns a score (1-8 points typically), requests are blocked when total score >= 5. Paranoia Levels (PL) trade coverage for false positives: PL1 is permissive (fewer false positives, misses some attacks), PL4 is strict (catches more attacks but may block legitimate users). Start at PL1 in detection mode (log and alert but do not block), then raise paranoia as you tune.</p>
    <p><strong>Failure mode: DNS cache poisoning and the Kaminsky attack.</strong> An off-path attacker (no visibility into the user's traffic) can forge DNS responses. The attacker races to send a forged response with the correct transaction ID before the real nameserver answers. The forged response redirects traffic to the attacker's IP. Defenses: source-port randomization (OS picks a random UDP source port for each query, not a fixed one; raises the entropy of the transaction ID from 16 bits to 32 bits, making brute-force harder). 0x20 encoding (randomize the capitalization of the domain name in the query — `ExAmPlE.cOm` — and verify the response matches the same capitalization; breaks naive off-path injection). DNSSEC (cryptographically sign responses at the zone level; requires the resolver to validate signatures; adoption is slow because many second-level domains lack DNSSEC records). In practice: modern resolvers enable source-port randomization by default, 0x20 adoption is under 0.4% globally, DNSSEC roots and most TLDs are signed but second-level adoption is patchy. The real protection is that large-scale botnets target HTTP/DNS amplification now, not individual cache poisoning, so the attack surface has shifted.</p>
    <p><strong>BGP hijacking and route leaks.</strong> BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is the protocol that announces IP address blocks across the Internet. An AS (Autonomous System) can be misconfigured to announce a prefix it does not own (route leak) or deliberately announce someone else's prefix (hijack), redirecting traffic. Defenses: RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure) — resource holders publish ROAs (Route Origin Authorizations) saying "AS 64512 is authorized to originate prefix 192.0.2.0/24." Routers do ROV (Route Origin Validation) — reject announcements from unauthorized ASes. As of 2026 about 54% of IPv4 and IPv6 prefixes have ROAs, but only ~25% of networks enforce ROV. BGPsec cryptographically signs the AS path, preventing path manipulation; adoption is minimal due to CPU overhead and operational complexity. MANRS (Mutually Agreed Norms for Routing Security) is an industry coalition of 71% of MANRS signers committing to ROV; it is a soft expectation without teeth but signals industry consensus.</p>
    <p><strong>False-positive vs coverage tradeoff.</strong> Stricter rules (higher paranoia level, lower anomaly-score threshold) catch more attacks but block more legitimate users. Looser rules let more traffic through but miss attacks. You cannot have both. Start in detection mode (log, do not block), measure the distribution of anomaly scores on legitimate traffic vs attack traffic, then pick a threshold that separates them well. The tradeoff is an operational decision: if a legitimate user cannot use your service because they are blocked by the WAF, you have a problem. If an attack slips through, you have a different problem. Most services err toward false negatives (letting some attacks through) because false positives erode user trust. Measure the ratio weekly and tune iteratively.</p>

    <p><strong>WAF tuning in production.</strong> WAFs ship with conservative default rules (Paranoia Level 1). Running PL1, you catch known patterns (SQL injection, XSS) with low false positives (~0.1-0.5%). But blind spots exist: novel attacks, obfuscation tricks, business-logic attacks (e.g., account enumeration via timing). Paranoia levels PL2-PL4 add stricter rules and anomaly-based scoring. The cost is higher false positives. To safely raise PL, run the WAF in detection mode (log, do not block) for 1-2 weeks. Collect baseline metrics on legitimate traffic: anomaly score distribution, rule hits per category, geolocation. Then raise the paranoia level and observe false-positive rate. If it exceeds acceptable threshold, tune rules: whitelist known false positives (e.g., legitimate uploads that trigger file-upload rules), adjust scoring weights, or exclude low-confidence rules. The goal is to find the paranoia level and score threshold that blocks 95%+ of attacks while affecting &lt;0.1% of real users.</p>

    <p><strong>Volumetric attack taxonomy in operational depth.</strong> UDP floods send millions of User Datagram Protocol packets per second; no handshake, no state tracking, purely bandwidth saturation. ICMP floods (ping floods) exploit the fact that routers must respond to ICMP echo-request messages. Memcached amplification (UDP mode): 40-byte SET/GET becomes 51,200-byte response = 1,280x amplification; mostly patched now but still exists on air-gapped networks.     <p><strong>Protocol/state-exhaustion attacks.</strong> SYN floods: attacker sends millions of SYN packets with spoofed source IPs or from a botnet, each one consuming a half-open connection slot in the server's SYN backlog. If backlog overflows, the server drops new SYN packets from legitimate clients. Kernel defense: SYN cookies (trade memory for CPU — drop the connection, encode the ISN as a cookie, re-allocate on ACK). ACK floods: send a million ACKs to a port no one is listening on. The kernel responds with RST packets, consuming CPU. Mitigated by TCP RST rate limiting (limit RSTs per second) and firewall-level suppression.</p>
    <p><strong>Rate-limiting algorithm internals.</strong> Token bucket: counter T (current tokens), rate R (refill per second), capacity C (max tokens). On each request: T = min(C, T + R * (time_since_last_request)). If T > 0, decrement T and allow; else reject. Distributed version (Redis backing) adds complexity: increment a counter atomically, check if it exceeds the limit. Leaky bucket: queue length Q, drain rate D. New request: if Q < max_queue then Q++; drain at D per second. Smooths traffic but adds latency. Fixed window: counter per second/minute; simple but hits the boundary-burst problem (two requests at 59s and two at 01s looks like four per window but is actually eight per two seconds). Sliding window counter: keep count of previous window and current window. On request at time T: allowed = (prev_count * (1 - T_elapsed/window_size)) + curr_count < limit. Blend of fixed-window efficiency and log accuracy. Distributed shared counter (multiple servers) requires coordination: Redis INCR is atomic but introduces latency. Modern systems use probabilistic counters (HyperLogLog) for approximate rate limiting with minimal overhead.</p>
    <p><strong>Adaptive and concurrency limiting.</strong> Instead of per-second limits, track in-flight request count (active connections). Set a max concurrency; new requests are rejected when in-flight >= max. Load shedding: when queue grows beyond a threshold (e.g., 1000 pending requests) begin rejecting new requests probabilistically. Survival probability = 1 / (queue_depth / max_queue). Users see fast rejections instead of timeout delays; the service stays responsive. Implement via circuit breakers (reject if too many recent failures) or health-based shedding (reject if latency p99 exceeds threshold). The cost is user-facing errors but the benefit is service stability under overload.</p>
    <p><strong>mTLS in service meshes with SPIFFE.</strong> Istio (Google, now CNCF) or Linkerd (Buoyant) deploy sidecar proxies on every pod. Control plane (e.g., Istiod) runs an SPIFFE-compatible certificate authority. At startup each sidecar receives a short-lived certificate (often 24 hours, though Google uses 1 hour). Certificate is rotated via SDS (Service Workload API) push — no sidecar restart needed. Every service-to-service call is:
    (1) mTLS handshake (20-50 ms overhead per new connection), 
    (2) encrypted communication, 
    (3) both sides verify certificates. Prevents lateral movement if the pod network is compromised (no service talks to another without cryptographic proof). The cost is observability burden (certs must be rotated, monitoring must track cert lifetimes, expired-cert incidents are infrastructure issues, not bugs). Netflix, Uber, and Google all run mTLS in production on service meshes.</p>
    <p><strong>TCP RST injection and RFC 5961.</strong> An attacker who cannot eavesdrop but can inject packets (on-path MITM) can forge a TCP RST with a sequence number in the receive window to tear down a connection. RFC 5961 mitigations: challenge-ACK (if RST sequence number is in-window but not exact, respond with ACK of the expected next byte instead of closing; forces the attacker to guess exactly or resend the RST), and ACK throttling (rate-limit challenge ACKs to ~10 per 5 seconds). Only defends against off-path attackers; on-path needs IPsec or TLS. Modern stacks implement RFC 5961 by default.</p>
    <p><strong>WAF detection modes and OWASP CRS.</strong> Signature-based: rules written by humans, e.g., "block if request contains 'union select' (SQL injection indicator)" or "block if URL contains '../' (path traversal)." Fast, no false positives on unknown traffic, but misses zero-days and obfuscated patterns (e.g., un/**/ion select). Anomaly/ML-based: train a model on normal traffic, flag statistical outliers. Catches novel attacks but requires careful tuning. OWASP Core Rule Set (CRS) uses both: signature rules detect known attacks, anomaly scoring assigns points. Rules score 1-8 points; request blocked when total &gt;= 5. Paranoia Levels (PL1-PL4) tune strictness: PL1 baseline (fewer false positives), PL4 strict (paranoid of everything). Start in detection mode (log, do not block), analyze scores on real traffic, then raise paranoia level or lower thresholds. Critical: measure false positive rate on legitimate users weekly and adjust. A 5% false positive rate (1 in 20 real users blocked) is unacceptable for public APIs; aim for &lt;0.1%.</p>
    <p><strong>BGP security in detail: RPKI, ROV, BGPsec, MANRS.</strong> RPKI (Resource Public Key Infrastructure): resource holders (RIRs, LIRs) publish ROAs (Route Origin Authorizations) in a public, cryptographically signed database. A ROA says "AS 64512 may originate prefix 192.0.2.0/24." Routers validate outgoing BGP announcements against ROAs: reject any announcement from an unauthorized AS (invalid), accept if authorized (valid), or ignore if ROA missing (unknown). As of 2026, ~54% of IPv4 and IPv6 prefixes have ROAs published, but only ~25% of networks enforce ROV (validation). BGPsec (IETF WG, not yet standardized for production) cryptographically signs the AS path, preventing path manipulation. CPU overhead is high; adoption minimal. MANRS (Mutually Agreed Norms for Routing Security) is an industry coalition (71% adoption among signers) committing to four practices: implement filters (don't announce prefixes you don't own), check ROAs (do ROV), maintain AS-WHOIS, and notify peering partners of hijacks. Soft pressure without enforcement, but signals industry maturity.</p>
    <p><strong>DNS security: Kaminsky attack and modern defenses.</strong> Kaminsky cache poisoning (2008): off-path attacker sends forged DNS responses racing the legit resolver's response. Query transaction ID is only 16 bits; with a powerful botnet the attacker can brute-force or guess within milliseconds. The attacker crafts queries asking for random subdomains (`a1s2d.example.com`, `xyz.example.com`, etc.) and races to poison the cache with a forged response mapping the subdomain to the attacker's IP. Once poisoned, all future lookups for example.com resolve to the attacker. Defenses: source-port randomization (query source port is random, not fixed; raises entropy from 16 bits to 32 bits, making guessing much harder). 0x20 encoding (randomize case in the query — `ExAmPlE.cOm` — and verify response matches; breaks naive injection because the attacker cannot predict the case). DNSSEC (sign each RRset with a public key, include signature in response, resolver validates before caching). Adoption: source-port randomization is default on modern resolvers; 0x20 adoption is under 0.4% globally; DNSSEC roots and most TLDs are signed, many second-level domains are not. Water-torture attacks (random-subdomain floods) now target resolvers more than cache poisoning — send thousands of queries for nonexistent subdomains to exhaust resolver CPU and memory.</p>
    <p><strong>Defense-in-depth architecture and anycast scrubbing.</strong> No single defense is sufficient. Layered approach: (1) anycast-based edge scrubbing — every PoP is a scrubbing center; traffic enters at the nearest PoP, not centralized; (2) rate limiting at the edge — reject obvious attack patterns; (3) WAF on origin or at edge — detect application-layer attacks; (4) firewall and DPI at origin — inspect traffic, block port scans and protocol violations; (5) observability and alerting — detect new attack patterns, escalate to humans. Cloudflare operates ~330+ PoPs in 120+ countries with ~37+ Tbps combined capacity. Akamai Prolexic runs ~20+ Tbps across scrubbing centers. Anycast means attack load is distributed across many nodes; one PoP absorbs a fraction of the attack. Contrast with the older model: reroute all traffic to a centralized scrubbing center (BGP failover), bottleneck risk.</p>
    <p><strong>Economics of attack vs defense.</strong> Attacker cost: ~$50-500/month for a botnet service (Mirai-class, 10 Gbps sustained). Origin defense cost: $500/month CDN bill for 10 Gbps sustained traffic. If the attacker can generate 100 Gbps and the origin pays only for 10 Gbps, the attacker wins on cost. But a CDN with global PoPs can ingest 100s of Tbps and spread the cost across 10,000+ customers, making the per-customer cost tiny. Large CDNs' scale advantage is the answer: individual services cannot afford 100+ Gbps capacity; CDNs amortize it. For a startup, DDoS defense is purely a CDN problem — you cannot solve it alone.</p>
    <p><strong>Observability at attack time.</strong> Metrics that matter: attack size (bits/sec, packets/sec, requests/sec), source geolocation (to detect spoofing), and fingerprints (identical User-Agents, Headers, Cookie names across sources). Tools: flow data (NetFlow, sFlow, IPFIX) tells you traffic distribution; packet captures (tcpdump) tell you headers; application logs tell you request content. During an attack, humans need to see: (1) request rate per second (normal ~1,000, under attack ~100,000), (2) geographic distribution of sources (all from one ASN = suspicious), (3) anomaly score distribution (normal users score &lt;1, attack traffic scores &gt;10), (4) cache hit rate (attack traffic often uncacheable). Alert thresholds: request rate 10x normal, or anomaly score 99th percentile spike, or source-IP entropy drop (all traffic from 100 different IPs instead of 100,000). Response: automatic rate limiting at edge, manual escalation to on-call engineer.</p>

<KeyTakeaway>
Network security requires defending three attack vectors: volumetric floods (L3/L4) absorbed at the CDN edge via stateless rate limiting and anycast distribution; protocol exhaustion (SYN floods, RST injection) blocked by kernel mitigations (SYN cookies, RFC 5961 challenge-ACK); and application-layer attacks (HTTP floods, Slowloris, expensive queries) detected by WAFs and rate limiters tuned to your traffic patterns. Defense architecture is layered (edge → gateway → origin) and operational (detect anomalies, tune rules, escalate to humans). The economics favor the attacker only if you defend alone; CDNs amortize cost across customers and make DDoS uneconomical.
</KeyTakeaway>
