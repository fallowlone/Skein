At the moment you click a link, your browser packages your request into an IP packet — an envelope with your address in it and the address of the server. But here's the radical part: no single router between you and that server knows the complete path. Each router just looks at the destination address, consults its own local map, and forwards the packet to the next hop. It works because every router does this independently, making greedy local decisions, and somehow the packet arrives.

<Crux>How does a packet find a server when no router knows the full route?</Crux>

<Misconception id="r-blackhole" title="Path MTU Discovery black-holes">
A router drops your packet and sends ICMP "packet too big," but a middlebox silently discards the ICMP. You never learn to shrink packets, retransmit at 1500 bytes forever, hitting ~1 second timeouts. TCP MSS clamping and assuming 1280-byte MTU mitigate this in practice.
</Misconception>

Every packet is born at the source and dies at the destination. In between, it's in flight, being read and forwarded by routers, each one making an independent decision about where it goes next. This is radically different from a circuit-switched telephone network, where a single call reserves a dedicated path end-to-end. IP is stateless and connectionless: routers don't care about the flow, and packets can take different paths, arrive out of order, or be dropped altogether. The next layer up — TCP — has to add reliability, ordering, and congestion control on top.
