You opened this page and a stranger on another continent agreed to talk to you. Before any HTTP request, before TLS, before DNS resolved — TCP did three things: said hello, agreed on starting sequence numbers, and confirmed the agreement. That's the handshake. Every byte that flows after rides on the state both sides built in those three packets.

<Crux>What SYN, SYN-ACK, and ACK actually negotiate — and why a connection's first RTT decides the speed of every request on it.</Crux>

The IP layer moved packets across networks, but it didn't care if they arrived, arrived in order, or arrived at all. TCP adds a contract: every byte is numbered, every byte is acknowledged, and if one is missing, it's retransmitted. That contract is negotiated in the handshake. <SpiralCue thread="statefulness" lang="en" /> — this is where statefulness earns its name. Before the handshake, both sides are strangers. After it, both sides carry state: buffers, windows, and the agreement to talk until one of them says goodbye.

    <p><strong>What TCP does in one sentence.</strong> It turns two strangers on the network into a reliable two-way conversation: every byte arrives in order, every byte is acknowledged, and missing bytes are resent. The handshake is how the conversation starts.</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Without TCP every web request would have to manage its own retries and ordering. TCP gives every higher protocol (HTTP, IMAP, SSH, anything that runs over it) a clean reliable pipe for free.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> Imagine two people on a phone call. Before they say anything important they exchange "hello — can you hear me? — yes, I can hear you, can you hear me? — yes." That short three-step ritual is the TCP handshake. SYN = "hello, here is my counter," SYN-ACK = "I hear you, here is my counter, and I confirm I heard your counter," ACK = "I confirm your counter, let's begin." Now both phones agree they are on the same line and can start the real conversation.</p>
    <p><strong>Read-aloud dialogue.</strong> <PersonaTag id="bea" lang="en" /> dials Sven and says "Hello, my counter starts at 42_000_000." <PersonaTag id="sven" lang="en" /> picks up: "Got it, your next byte should be 42_000_001; my counter starts at 99_000_000." Bea: "Got it, your next byte should be 99_000_001, let's go." From this moment every word both of them say is numbered so neither can lose track.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario end to end.</strong> You click a link in your browser. Underneath, the browser already finished <a href="/en/networking/02-ip-packet/">IP routing</a> (it knows the server's address) and <a href="/en/networking/04-dns-resolution/">DNS</a> (it resolved the name). Now it does the TCP handshake you just learned. Once finished, the browser layers <a href="/en/networking/05-tls-handshake/">TLS</a> on top to seal the connection, and only then does the actual <a href="/en/networking/06-http-versions/">HTTP</a> request travel.</p>

  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p><strong>The three-step dance.</strong> Bea sends <code>SYN seq=X</code>. She moves to state <code>SYN-SENT</code>. Sven receives it, allocates a buffer for the incoming direction, moves to state <code>SYN-RECV</code>, and replies <code>SYN-ACK seq=Y ack=X+1</code>. The <code>ack=X+1</code> means "I saw your sequence number X; the next byte I expect from you is X+1." (The SYN flag itself consumes one sequence number.) Bea then sends <code>ACK seq=X+1 ack=Y+1</code> and moves to state <code>ESTABLISHED</code>. Sven receives it, moves to state <code>ESTABLISHED</code>, and the connection is live.</p>
    <p><strong>Why three steps and not two?</strong> Because TCP is bidirectional and <strong>both sides must agree on each other's starting sequence numbers.</strong> If Bea sent SYN and then immediately started sending data, Sven would allocate state on receiving SYN, but what if that SYN was a retransmitted packet from an old, crashed connection? Sven needs Bea to acknowledge his sequence number before she sends data, proving she actually received and understood his SYN-ACK. That acknowledgement is the third step. It also bounds half-open connections: if a SYN arrives but no ACK follows, the server times out the half-open entry and frees memory. This defends against SYN floods.</p>
    <p><strong>Initial Sequence Numbers (ISN).</strong> RFC 6528 specifies that ISN is computed as ISN = M + F(saddr, sport, daddr, dport, secret_key), where M increments once per ~4 µs (making it hard to guess), and F is a hash of the 4-tuple and secret key. Older implementations used a simple counter, which attackers exploited: an attacker could guess the next ISN, forge a packet with the right sequence number, and inject commands into an established connection. Modern ISNs are cryptographically random per connection, making blind injection impossible without eavesdropping.</p>
    <p><strong>The cost: one full RTT.</strong> Bea sends SYN, waits for SYN-ACK (1 RTT), sends ACK, and only then can she send an HTTP request. The HTTP request sits in the ACK packet or arrives right after. If the RTT is 100 ms (e.g., across a continent), 100 ms of latency is baked into the connection before the first application byte lands. <SpiralCue thread="latency" lang="en" /> — this RTT matters because it decides the cadence of the entire application.</p>
    <p><strong>Sliding window and flow control.</strong> Once established, every TCP segment carries a window size — the number of bytes the receiver is willing to accept beyond the last acknowledged byte. The sender keeps a sliding window of unacknowledged bytes in flight, bounded by the minimum of the receiver's advertised window and its own congestion window. As ACKs arrive the window slides forward, allowing new bytes. If the receiver's application is slow to drain the socket buffer, the window shrinks; if the buffer fills entirely the receiver advertises window=0 and the sender pauses. This is end-to-end flow control: the receiver dictates the pace, the sender obeys.</p>
    <p><strong>MSS, window scaling, and SACK.</strong> The Maximum Segment Size (MSS) option in SYN tells the peer the largest TCP payload it can handle without IP fragmentation; typical values are 1460 bytes on Ethernet (1500 MTU minus 20 IP + 20 TCP headers) or 1220 on tunnelled networks. Window Scaling (RFC 7323) multiplies the 16-bit window field by 2^scale, allowing windows up to 1 GiB — essential on high-bandwidth links because raw 64 KiB caps throughput badly on long-RTT paths. Selective Acknowledgements (SACK, RFC 2018) let the receiver tell the sender exactly which segments arrived even when there is a hole, so the sender retransmits only the missing pieces instead of everything after the gap.</p>
    <p><strong>Connection close: FIN and TIME-WAIT.</strong> Closing is the mirror of opening: each side independently sends FIN to signal "I have no more data," and the peer ACKs. Connections sit in TIME-WAIT for 2×MSL (~120s typical) so any straggler retransmission can land harmlessly instead of being mistaken for a new connection's data. The side that initiates close pays the TIME-WAIT cost; on busy clients this is fine, on busy servers it becomes a problem — see TIME-WAIT exhaustion in the senior tier.</p>
    <p><strong>Retransmit basics.</strong> If a segment goes un-ACKed within the retransmission timeout (RTO), the sender resends it. RFC 6298 specifies RTO = SRTT + max(G, 4×RTTVAR), where SRTT (smoothed RTT) and RTTVAR (RTT variance) are EWMAs updated on every ACK. The first RTO defaults to 1s; after a timeout it doubles (exponential backoff) until either the segment arrives or the connection gives up. Modern Linux uses RACK-TLP (RFC 8985) for faster loss detection without waiting for the RTO timer in many cases.</p>
    <p><strong>Slow start and congestion avoidance.</strong> A fresh connection has no idea how fast the network is. Slow start opens the congestion window exponentially (1, 2, 4, 8, ... MSS per RTT) until either a loss occurs or the slow-start threshold (ssthresh) is reached. After that, congestion avoidance grows the window linearly (~1 MSS per RTT for Reno/CUBIC). On loss, the algorithm differs by variant: Reno halves the window, CUBIC reduces it less aggressively then probes back with a cubic curve, and BBR ignores loss entirely as a congestion signal and uses RTT + delivered-bytes measurements instead.</p>
    <p><strong>Nagle's algorithm and delayed ACK.</strong> By default a TCP sender batches small writes via Nagle's algorithm: do not send a partial segment if there is unacknowledged data in flight. Independently, the receiver delays ACKs up to 40 ms (Linux default) hoping to piggyback the ACK on outgoing application data. Combined, these two cause a 200 ms+ stall on small request-response patterns. <code>TCP_NODELAY</code> disables Nagle on a socket; <code>TCP_QUICKACK</code> disables delayed ACK temporarily; the canonical fix for low-latency RPC traffic is to set <code>TCP_NODELAY</code> on every socket.</p>
    <p><strong>State diagram in practice.</strong> The standard states you will see in <code>ss -tan</code> output: <code>LISTEN</code> (server waiting for SYN), <code>SYN-SENT</code> (client sent SYN), <code>SYN-RECV</code> (server sent SYN-ACK), <code>ESTABLISHED</code> (handshake done, normal traffic), <code>FIN-WAIT-1</code> / <code>FIN-WAIT-2</code> / <code>CLOSING</code> / <code>LAST-ACK</code> (graceful close states), <code>TIME-WAIT</code> (post-close cooldown). An accumulation of <code>CLOSE-WAIT</code> on a server usually means the application is not calling <code>close()</code> on accepted sockets — a leak.</p>
    <p><strong>Keepalive.</strong> By default a TCP connection sends no packets when idle, so a connection through a NAT or firewall may be silently dropped after 5–60 minutes. <code>SO_KEEPALIVE</code> (or HTTP/2 ping frames at higher layers) sends a probe every <code>tcp_keepalive_time</code> seconds (Linux default 7200 s = 2h, far too long). For service-mesh or long-lived RPC connections, tune this to 60–120 s and combine with <code>tcp_keepalive_intvl</code> = 10–30 s and <code>tcp_keepalive_probes</code> = 3–5 so dead peers are detected within a minute.</p>
    <p><strong>Misconception block.</strong> Three things commonly confused at this tier. One: "TCP guarantees delivery." It guarantees delivery <em>or</em> connection failure — the application sees clean error semantics, not silent loss. Two: "Window scaling is automatic everywhere." False — some old middleboxes strip the option, capping windows at 64 KiB and crippling throughput on intercontinental links. Three: "SYN cookies have no downside." They drop most TCP options (window scaling, SACK, timestamps) for the protected connection, hurting throughput; only valuable as a SYN-flood defence.</p>
    <p><strong>TCP header anatomy.</strong> Every TCP segment carries a 20-byte mandatory header plus optional fields up to 40 bytes more. Mandatory: source port (16 bits) + destination port (16 bits) — the four-tuple component that distinguishes connections sharing one host. Sequence number (32 bits) — position of the first data byte in the segment within the stream. Acknowledgement number (32 bits) — the next byte the sender expects from the peer. Data offset (4 bits) — header length in 32-bit words, because options vary. Flags (8 bits): URG, ACK, PSH, RST, SYN, FIN, CWR, ECE. Window (16 bits) — bytes the receiver is willing to accept. Checksum (16 bits) — covers a pseudo-header from IP plus the TCP segment. Urgent pointer (16 bits) — almost never used in practice; relic of old terminal protocols.</p>
    <p><strong>PSH flag and small-message latency.</strong> The PSH (push) flag tells the receiver's TCP stack to deliver buffered data to the application immediately rather than waiting for more. In practice it is set on the last segment of every write() that completes a logical message, and modern stacks pass data up to the application as soon as it arrives anyway, so PSH is more a hint than a mandate. The interesting case is when the kernel's TCP stack coalesces multiple application writes into one segment — PSH ensures the receiver still sees a single deliverable record even after coalescing.</p>
    <p><strong>Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN).</strong> Instead of dropping packets to signal congestion, ECN-capable routers mark them with a 2-bit code (CE = Congestion Experienced) and let TCP endpoints reduce their send rate without retransmission. The TCP header reserves CWR (Congestion Window Reduced) and ECE (ECN-Echo) flags for the endpoint protocol. ECN negotiation happens in the SYN/SYN-ACK exchange: both sides advertise capability via the ECE+CWR flags during the handshake. Adoption has been slow due to broken middleboxes that drop ECN-marked packets, but ECN is now enabled by default in Linux and macOS for connections to known-good destinations.</p>
    <p><strong>TCP options field.</strong> Beyond MSS, Window Scale, and SACK described above, the options field can carry Timestamps (RFC 7323) used for both round-trip measurement and PAWS (Protection Against Wrapped Sequence numbers — preventing old delayed packets from being mistaken for new data on long-lived high-bandwidth connections). The TFO cookie also lives here. Options are negotiated only in the SYN/SYN-ACK; once the connection is established the option set is fixed. Maximum options size is 40 bytes (header data offset field limits total header to 60 bytes minus the 20 mandatory).</p>
    <p><strong>SACK in action.</strong> Without SACK, a single missing segment forces the receiver to keep ACKing the last good byte even as later segments arrive. The sender sees duplicate ACKs (three of them trigger fast retransmit per Reno) and retransmits the missing segment, but does not know which later segments arrived — it may retransmit everything after the gap. With SACK, the receiver lists ranges of bytes successfully received past the gap (up to 4 SACK blocks per segment per RFC 2018), so the sender retransmits only the actually missing pieces. On a lossy long-RTT path SACK can double effective throughput.</p>
    <p><strong>Congestion control variants summarised.</strong> Reno (the classical algorithm): halve cwnd on loss, additive increase per RTT. New Reno: better recovery from multiple losses within one window. CUBIC (Linux default since 2.6.19): cubic-curve growth function — concave probe before reaching the previous max, convex probe past it; quickly recovers throughput on high-BDP paths. BBR: ignore loss as a congestion signal, model bottleneck-bandwidth and minimum-RTT directly. Each variant is selectable per-socket via TCP_CONGESTION sockopt or system-wide via sysctl. The default choice rarely matters until your service hits a specific operational ceiling — at that point the senior tier becomes urgent reading.</p>

  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p><strong>TCP state machine.</strong> The full diagram has CLOSED, LISTEN, SYN-SENT, SYN-RECV, ESTABLISHED, FIN-WAIT-1, FIN-WAIT-2, CLOSE-WAIT, CLOSING, TIME-WAIT, and LAST-ACK. On the server: LISTEN → SYN-RECV → ESTABLISHED. On the client: CLOSED → SYN-SENT → ESTABLISHED. Most connections stay in ESTABLISHED until FIN closes them. TIME-WAIT persists for 2 × MSL (Maximum Segment Lifetime, typically 60s each → ~120s total) after the final ACK to allow delayed retransmitted FINs to be absorbed. This is why restarting a server immediately after shutdown can fail with "address already in use" — the port is still in TIME-WAIT.</p>
    <p><strong>TCP Fast Open (TFO, RFC 7413).</strong> The standard handshake eats one RTT before application data. TFO caches an opaque cookie from the first handshake and allows the client to send data in the SYN packet on subsequent connections to the same server. The server validates the cookie, and if it's valid, the connection is established without waiting for the ACK. This reduces the handshake from 1 RTT to 0 RTT for the data. Cold start (first connection) is still 1 RTT, but warm start (cached cookie) cuts the delay. Modern major sites (Google, Facebook, etc.) enable TFO. Deployment risk: some middleboxes strip the TFO option from the SYN, reverting the connection to standard 1-RTT behavior.</p>
    <p><strong>SYN cookies (RFC 4987).</strong> Under a SYN flood, the server's queue of half-open connections overflows. Instead of allocating memory for every SYN, the kernel can compute a cryptographic cookie from the client's IP:port and the server's IP:port, send it back as the sequence number in SYN-ACK, and immediately forget the connection. When the client sends ACK, the server recomputes the cookie to verify it, then allocates state. The cost is CPU: the server checks the cookie on every ACK. This trades memory for CPU — fine for large servers, not for small ones. Linux enables SYN cookies when the half-open queue exceeds <code>tcp_max_syn_backlog</code> (system-dependent; scales with memory, ~128 to 4096+).</p>
    <p><strong>Bufferbloat at the handshake.</strong> On high-latency, high-bandwidth links, the handshake completes quickly, but the first data-sending window is large. TCP slow-start opens the congestion window exponentially (1, 2, 4, 8, … segments per RTT), but if the buffer is large (100 ms of packets), queuing delay compounds. BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and RTT, RFC 7748) models the bottleneck explicitly and opens more conservatively, reducing queueing latency.</p>
    <p><strong>Linux tunables:</strong> <code>tcp_syncookies</code> (0/1/2 — disabled/enabled/enabled-passive), <code>tcp_max_syn_backlog</code> (default 4096), <code>net.ipv4.tcp_fastopen</code> (bitmask: bit 0 = client, bit 1 = server), <code>tcp_tw_reuse</code> and <code>tcp_tw_recycle</code> (reuse/recycle TIME-WAIT). Changing these on production without testing can break connections, so observability (netlstat, ss, BPF tracing) is critical.</p>
    <p><strong>BBR vs CUBIC vs Reno in 2026.</strong> CUBIC has been the Linux default since 2.6.19 (2006) and works well on terrestrial links with deep buffers; it reacts to loss by reducing the window with a cubic curve and probes back aggressively after recovery. BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and RTT) abandons loss-based signalling entirely — it estimates the path's bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT directly and paces sends to match, treating loss as ambiguous noise. BBRv3 (Linux 6.x series, deployed across google.com and YouTube as of 2024-2026) fixes BBRv2's premature-probe and convergence bugs and is the default on all Google public traffic. Cloudflare and Netflix run BBR on their edges. The pragmatic guidance: stick with CUBIC for general-purpose servers, switch to BBR for cross-continental WAN or high-latency cellular paths where CUBIC's loss-driven cuts collapse throughput. Set per-socket via <code>TCP_CONGESTION</code> sockopt or system-wide via <code>net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control</code>.</p>
    <p><strong>SYN cookies internals.</strong> When the SYN queue overflows, the kernel takes the path in <code>net/ipv4/tcp_ipv4.c:cookie_v4_init_sequence</code> instead of allocating a request_sock. The ISN it returns in SYN-ACK is a MAC: top 5 bits = MSS index (8 options), next 6 bits = minute counter for replay window, low 21 bits = HMAC over (saddr, sport, daddr, dport, isn, secret). When the ACK comes back, <code>cookie_v4_check</code> recomputes and validates. The trade-off: only MSS survives in the cookie. Window scaling, SACK, timestamps, and TFO are silently dropped for cookie-validated connections, so throughput suffers on long-RTT links during a flood. Enable with <code>net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1</code> (default since 2.4); value <code>2</code> forces unconditional cookie generation for testing.</p>
    <p><strong>TIME-WAIT exhaustion at scale.</strong> Linux ephemeral port range defaults to 32768–60999 (~28k ports). A busy client opening 1000 short HTTP/1.1 connections per second to one origin burns through that pool in 28 seconds; with 2×MSL = 60–120 s of TIME-WAIT before reuse, the pool exhausts and <code>connect()</code> returns EADDRNOTAVAIL. Three production fixes: (1) <code>net.ipv4.tcp_tw_reuse=1</code> reuses TIME-WAIT sockets going outbound when timestamps prove the new connection is valid (RFC 6191 / PAWS); (2) widen <code>net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range</code> to <code>1024 65535</code> for ~64k ports; (3) the real fix: use keep-alive / HTTP/2 / connection pools so you don't burn a four-tuple per request. Do NOT enable <code>tcp_tw_recycle</code> — it was removed in Linux 4.12 because it breaks NAT.</p>
    <p><strong>Retransmission timer math (RFC 6298).</strong> SRTT (smoothed RTT) and RTTVAR (RTT variance) are exponentially-weighted moving averages: SRTT ← (1 − α)·SRTT + α·new_RTT with α = 1/8; RTTVAR ← (1 − β)·RTTVAR + β·|SRTT − new_RTT| with β = 1/4. RTO = SRTT + max(G, 4·RTTVAR), bounded below by 1 s (Linux often 200 ms) and above by 60 s. Karn's algorithm forbids sampling RTT from retransmitted segments because the responding ACK is ambiguous (does it acknowledge the first send or the retransmission?). Modern Linux uses RACK-TLP (RFC 8985) on top: RACK declares a segment lost when a later segment was ACKed and a reorder-window has expired, without waiting for the RTO; TLP (Tail Loss Probe) re-sends the last unacknowledged segment one RTT after the last send to avoid hanging on the final packet of a flight.</p>
    <p><strong>TCP Fast Open (TFO, RFC 7413) deployment reality.</strong> First connection: server sends a Fast Open Cookie option in SYN-ACK; client caches per-(server-IP, server-port). Subsequent connections: client puts the cookie back in SYN along with up to MSS bytes of application data; server validates and processes the data while completing the handshake. Saves 1 RTT — meaningful on intercontinental links. Hurdles: middleboxes that don't recognise the option strip it; some NAT gateways break the cookie because the client's external IP changes; falling back is mandatory (client must detect timeout and retry without TFO). Linux supports both sides via <code>net.ipv4.tcp_fastopen</code> bitmask (1 = client, 2 = server, 3 = both). Real-world deployment is mostly client-side; very few servers enable it because the gain on warm pools is small versus QUIC's 0-RTT.</p>
    <p><strong>Nagle + delayed ACK pathology.</strong> Default behaviour on both sides creates a deadly handshake: Nagle holds the sender's small write because an earlier segment is unacknowledged; the receiver holds the ACK up to 40 ms hoping to piggyback on outgoing data; result is a fixed ~200 ms hang on every interactive RPC. The fix is to set <code>TCP_NODELAY=1</code> on the socket; gRPC, HTTP/2 implementations, Redis clients, and any latency-sensitive RPC layer enable it by default. <code>TCP_QUICKACK=1</code> tells Linux to ACK the next segment immediately (it auto-resets after one packet so call it after each <code>read()</code> in a tight loop). The combined fix on a service-mesh sidecar can recover tens of milliseconds per request.</p>
    <p><strong>kTLS + TCP integration.</strong> kTLS (Linux 4.13+ TX, 4.17+ RX, NIC offload on 6.0+) bolts the symmetric TLS record layer onto a TCP socket via <code>setsockopt(SOL_TLS, ...)</code>. After the user-space TLS handshake completes the kernel takes over record encryption; combined with <code>sendfile()</code> the file moves page-cache → NIC without ever entering user space — Netflix reports 8–29% CPU savings on static asset delivery. kTLS does not change TCP behaviour: congestion control, retransmits, window management all remain in the standard TCP stack. The integration point is purely the encryption layer; everything you know about TCP keeps working.</p>
    <p><strong>MPTCP (RFC 8684).</strong> Multipath TCP carries one logical connection across multiple paths (Wi-Fi + cellular, multi-NIC server). The MPTCP handshake adds an MP_CAPABLE option in SYN/SYN-ACK/ACK; if both ends support it the first sub-flow is established, and additional sub-flows can be opened on different network interfaces and joined to the same connection via MP_JOIN. iOS uses MPTCP since iOS 7 for Siri (seamless cellular-Wi-Fi handover); Linux 5.6+ ships RFC 8684. Limited adoption outside Apple because middleboxes that don't understand the option fall back to plain TCP on one path.</p>
    <p><strong>Production observability.</strong> <code>ss -tin</code> dumps live cwnd, RTT, RTT variance, retransmits, and backoff state per connection — no kernel overhead, run it on any production host. <code>ss -s</code> summarises socket counts by state (watch CLOSE-WAIT spikes). <code>tcpdump -i any -w trace.pcap port 443</code> captures raw packets for offline analysis. <code>nstat</code> exposes counters from <code>/proc/net/netstat</code> (RetransSegs, TCPSlowStartRetrans, TCPDSACKRecv). For long-term monitoring, the <code>tcp_diag</code> netlink interface feeds Prometheus exporters (node_exporter exposes most of these); the metrics that matter for SLOs are retransmission rate, p95/p99 RTT, and the ratio of FIN-WAIT-2 to ESTABLISHED (high ratio = connection-close mismatch with the peer).</p>
    <p><strong>Reset (RST) semantics.</strong> A TCP RST is an abrupt connection close: no FIN exchange, no TIME-WAIT, the receiver drops the connection state immediately. It happens when (a) a packet arrives for a port no one is listening on, (b) the application calls <code>close()</code> on a socket with unread data and <code>SO_LINGER</code> with lingertime=0, (c) the peer sends garbage that violates the state machine, or (d) a stateful firewall decides the connection is idle. RST attacks are real: an attacker who can guess sequence numbers within the receive window can forge an RST and tear down an established connection. Modern stacks tighten the acceptable RST window (RFC 5961) but the attack surface persists on long-lived idle connections like BGP sessions and SSH.</p>
    <p><strong>Cross-protocol layering.</strong> TCP is one layer in the stack; TLS sits directly on top and inherits TCP's reliability + ordering; HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2 ride on TLS; HTTP/3 is the exception — it runs on QUIC, which uses UDP and reinvents reliability + congestion control in user space because evolving TCP in the kernel turned out to be too slow. The lessons of TCP — sequence numbers, ACKs, congestion control, slow start, fast retransmit — all reappear in QUIC, just at a different layer. Understanding TCP makes QUIC trivial; the inverse is not true.</p>

<Misconception
  id="r-syn-flood"
  title="Heads-up: SYN floods aren't dead"
>
SYN cookies mitigate classic SYN floods but ignore TCP options like timestamps. Modern attacks layer SYN floods with TLS ClientHellos, starving handshake CPU. Defence is layered: rate limits, connection priming, and abuse-pattern detection.
</Misconception>

The handshake is the first contract in the stack, and it's expensive. On intercontinental links (100–300 ms RTT), you pay that cost for every new connection. This is why <SpiralCue thread="latency" lang="en" /> — connection reuse, pipelining, and keep-alive are non-optional optimizations. HTTP/1.1 added Keep-Alive to hold connections open across multiple requests. HTTP/2 multiplexes many requests over one connection. QUIC and TLS 1.3 cache session state so the second connection completes faster or with 0-RTT resumption. But the first connection, in every case, pays the handshake tax. Engineers design around it by pooling connections, warming connections in the background, and accepting that the coldest request will always be slow.

<KeyTakeaway>
TCP's three-way handshake exchanges and acknowledges starting sequence numbers in both directions before any byte of application data flows, costing one full RTT per connection. Modern Linux protects the handshake with SYN cookies under flood, replaces the loss-based congestion control of Reno with CUBIC or BBR for lossy long-RTT paths, and uses RACK-TLP to detect loss faster than the classic RTO timer. Above the protocol layer the operational concerns are TIME-WAIT exhaustion (fix with connection pools + tcp_tw_reuse), Nagle/delayed-ACK stalls (fix with TCP_NODELAY), and kernel-tunable hygiene for any service handling thousands of handshakes per second.
</KeyTakeaway>
