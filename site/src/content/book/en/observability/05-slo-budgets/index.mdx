The previous piece turned RED into the symptom dashboard. This piece turns the same numbers into a contract you can spend. An SLO is not a wish; it is a target percentage that names exactly how many failed requests the business will tolerate, and an error budget is the remainder — the failures you are *allowed* to produce before the deployment freeze kicks in. Done well, it ends arguments about "how reliable is reliable enough?" and replaces them with arithmetic.

<Crux>SLI = good/total. SLO = target SLI. Error budget = 1 - SLO. Burn rate = how fast you're spending it.</Crux>

The mental model is financial. A 99.9% SLO means you have a 0.1% allowance of failures. Over 30 days, that is 43.2 minutes of downtime, or 1,000 errored requests per 1,000,000 served. As real failures happen, the budget drains; when it goes negative, you halt feature work and spend cycles on reliability until you're back in the black. The alerting layer sits on top: multi-window multi-burn-rate alerts page when the budget is being consumed fast enough that you'll run out before the period ends, and ignore the slow trickle that's within tolerance. Senior engineers internalise this loop because it is the single most leveraged cultural mechanism in production reliability — it turns "should we ship this feature or fix this bug?" into a number, not an argument.

    <p><strong>What this is in one sentence.</strong> An SLO is a promise about how reliable your service will be, written as a percentage, and an error budget is the leftover failure allowance you can spend before that promise is broken.</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Without an SLO, reliability talk is opinion: someone wants "more reliable", someone wants "ship faster". With an SLO the numbers decide.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> Think of the error budget like a monthly data plan on your phone. 99.9% reliability over 30 days is the same shape as "10 GB per month": you start the month with a fixed allowance, you spend it as you go, and if you run out before month-end, you slow down (no new feature deploys) until the next billing cycle. A tiny incident is like a 30-second video — barely a dent. A two-hour outage is like streaming HD all weekend — that's most of the month's quota gone. Heavy spenders ration; light spenders ship.</p>
    <p><strong>Bea and Sven.</strong> <PersonaTag id="bea" lang="en" /> the product manager asks: «can we ship the new checkout flow this week?» <PersonaTag id="sven" lang="en" /> the SRE dashboard answers: this month we promised 99.9% checkout availability; we've burned 60% of the budget in the first 10 days because of last week's database incident. They agree to ship behind a feature flag, watch the burn rate for 24 hours, and roll forward only if the budget keeps draining at the baseline.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario.</strong> A SaaS team sets a 99.5% SLO. Over a month they serve 10M requests; 0.5% = 50,000 errors allowed. A week-one config bug burns 30,000 errors in 20 minutes — 60% of the budget gone in one incident. Burn-rate alert fired; the postmortem used the burned budget as the severity metric.</p>

  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p><strong>SLI: pick a ratio that actually tracks user pain.</strong> The Google SRE workbook is firm: a good SLI is a ratio of good events to total events, in [0%, 100%], that correlates with what users feel. For request-driven services that usually means availability (successful_requests / total_requests, filtering 5xx) and latency (requests_below_threshold / total_requests, e.g. fast_requests with a 200ms cutoff). For pipelines: throughput (records_processed / records_arrived) and freshness (records_processed_within_SLA / total). For storage: durability (data_intact / data_stored) and latency (reads_under_threshold / total_reads). The forbidden anti-pattern is "internal SLI": CPU usage, queue length, GC pauses — these are USE-style operational signals, not SLIs, because they don't directly correlate with user happiness. A 100% CPU box may be serving every request perfectly; an SLI on CPU would scream during healthy autoscaling events.</p>
    <p><strong>SLO: the target percentage.</strong> The number is a business call, not an engineering call. 99.9% over 30 days means 43.2 minutes of downtime allowed; 99.95% means 21.6 minutes; 99.99% means 4.3 minutes; 99.999% means 26 seconds. Each extra nine roughly multiplies engineering cost 3-10x: 99.9% is a single-region deployment with monitoring; 99.99% needs multi-region with automated failover; 99.999% needs paranoid redundancy, chaos engineering, and a 24/7 on-call rotation that wakes up within minutes. The right SLO is the one where the cost of the next nine exceeds the user's willingness to pay for it. Most product teams over-set: 99.99% sounds impressive in the meeting, but the business doesn't actually need it, and the team burns out chasing a number that no user cares about.</p>
    <p><strong>The error budget arithmetic.</strong> Error budget = (1 − SLO) × total_events_in_window. For a 99.9% SLO over 30 days at 1M requests/day, total events = 30M, budget = 30,000 failed requests. As real failures accumulate, budget remaining = budget_total − failures_so_far. Burn rate at any moment = (current_failure_rate / (1 − SLO)). A 0.1% error rate at a 99.9% SLO is a burn rate of 1: you'd consume the whole budget exactly by the end of the period. A 1.44% error rate is a burn rate of 14.4: you'd consume the entire 30-day budget in 30 / 14.4 ≈ 2.08 days. The burn rate is the single most useful derived quantity in SLO monitoring; it normalises the noise across services with different SLOs and traffic volumes.</p>
    <p><strong>Rolling window: why 28 days, not "this month".</strong> Calendar months vary (28-31 days) and create cliff effects: a bad week at the end of February has a different budget impact than the same bad week at the end of January. A rolling window — usually 28 days (four whole weeks) — solves both: it always covers the same length, and it includes full weekday/weekend cycles so traffic patterns normalise. Some teams use 7-day windows for very fast iteration; some use quarterly for slow-moving infrastructure. The window length is a knob, but 28 days is the workhorse default in production SLO platforms (Sloth, Pyrra, Datadog SLOs, Nobl9, Google Cloud SLOs, Splunk).</p>
    <p><strong>Multi-window multi-burn-rate alerting.</strong> Naive SLO alerts come in two broken flavours: alert on instantaneous error rate over SLO (Approach 1 in the SRE workbook) — which fires hundreds of times a day on tiny blips and trains the team to ignore the pager; or alert on long-window burn (Approach 5) — which takes hours to fire on a real outage. The workbook's recommended Approach 6 (multi-window multi-burn-rate, MWMBR) combines a short window and a long window per severity level, and only fires when *both* exceed the threshold. For a 99.9% SLO the canonical configuration is:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Page severity:</strong> 1-hour window AND 5-minute window both at &gt;14.4x burn rate (consumes 2% of the 30-day budget in 1 hour). Fast detection on big outages; the 5-min window confirms the spike is still ongoing.</li>
      <li><strong>Page severity:</strong> 6-hour window AND 30-minute window both at &gt;6x burn rate (consumes 5% of budget in 6 hours). Catches sustained moderate burns that the 1-hour window would miss.</li>
      <li><strong>Ticket severity:</strong> 3-day window AND 6-hour window both at &gt;1x burn rate (consumes 10% of budget in 3 days). Slow burns that need attention but don't need to wake anyone.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>The dual-window trick is the magic: a single-window alert that fires at 14.4x burn over 1 hour keeps firing for an hour after the issue is resolved (because the window still contains the bad data). Adding a 5-minute confirmation window lets the alert clear within 5 minutes of resolution. Detection time and reset time are both bounded; engineers stop normalising the noise.</p>
    <p><strong>The error budget policy.</strong> The policy is a written, signed document that says: when budget is exhausted, deployments halt except for P0 fixes and security patches, and the team focuses on reliability work until the budget regenerates above some threshold (typically 50% remaining). Beyond the freeze, mature policies include: postmortem requirement if a single incident burns &gt; 20% of the budget; quarterly review of the SLO itself; escalation to the CTO or VP if the freeze lasts more than two weeks; exemption clauses for upstream infrastructure failures and out-of-scope errors (load tests, penetration tests, miscategorised events). The signatures matter — without a director-level signer, the policy is a suggestion the on-call team is pressured to ignore during release crunches. With a signer, it is a covenant.</p>
    <p><strong>Latency SLOs use bucketed counts, not percentiles directly.</strong> An availability SLO is trivially a ratio of counts: 5xx_count / total_count. A latency SLO needs to express "X% of requests under Y ms" — which sounds like a percentile but is implemented as a counter: fast_requests_total (requests with duration &lt; SLO_threshold) divided by total_requests_total. The histogram bucket at the SLO threshold gives this counter directly, no <code>histogram_quantile</code> required. This is why the RED-Duration histogram must have a bucket boundary exactly at the SLO target: without it, you cannot evaluate the SLO without estimating, and estimates contaminate the budget calculation. Production-grade SLO instrumentation always defines the histogram buckets around the SLO before computing anything else.</p>
    <p><strong>Composite SLOs and worst-of joins.</strong> A user-facing journey often spans multiple services: a checkout request hits API gateway → auth → inventory → payment → database. Each service has its own SLO. The journey-level SLO is the conjunction: a request is "good" only if every service it touched was good. If each service has 99.9% availability and a request hits five services, the journey-level availability is 0.999⁵ ≈ 99.5%. Teams routinely miss this multiplication; their per-service dashboards look healthy while the user-facing dashboard shows misery. Production-grade SLO platforms support composite SLOs (worst-of-N joins across services) so the user-journey SLO is computed correctly and budgets are spent at the right level.</p>
    <p><strong>SLO platforms: don't hand-roll the rules.</strong> The amount of PromQL needed to express an MWMBR alert correctly is substantial — six windows × per-window recording rules × per-SLO targets × per-service replication, all consistent. Hand-rolled implementations diverge across teams within months; one team's "burn rate" is computed differently from another's, dashboards lie, alerts misfire. The standard fix is a generator: Sloth (open source, YAML → Prometheus rules), Pyrra (similar, with a Kubernetes operator), OpenSLO (the cross-tool standard spec), Nobl9 (commercial), Datadog SLOs (managed). You write the SLO declaratively — SLI definition, target, window, severity — and the tool generates the recording rules, the alerts, the dashboards, and the documentation. Adoption matters more than choice of tool: any of these is better than hand-rolled, because the consistency is what turns SLOs from individual heroics into org-wide culture. A platform team's first SLO-related task is almost always "pick one generator, mandate it, deprecate the bespoke YAMLs."</p>
    <p><strong>The first 90 days of an SLO rollout.</strong> A common pattern when a team adopts SLOs: week 1-2, pick the first user journey and define SLIs at the API gateway. Week 3-4, set a conservative SLO target (looser than you think — you can always tighten). Week 5-8, instrument the counters with the right histogram buckets, generate the MWMBR alerts via Sloth, watch what fires for a few weeks without acting on them (silent mode). Week 9-12, write the error budget policy, get director sign-off, start treating alerts as real. The trap teams fall into: setting the SLO too tight on day one, getting flooded with alerts they can't act on, and abandoning the system before the policy is even signed. Start loose, tighten quarterly. The SLO target is a knob, not a verdict; the value comes from the discipline of having it, not from any particular number.</p>

  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p><strong>Edge case: low-traffic services break naive SLOs.</strong> A service serving 100 requests an hour has SLO arithmetic problems. A 99.9% SLO at that volume is one allowed failure per 1000 requests — but 1000 requests takes 10 hours of real time. A single failure during a 1-hour alerting window is the entire hourly traffic plus some; you're at 100% error rate by the math. Solutions: synthetic traffic (generate consistent probe requests so the denominator never drops too low), aggregation across logically-related services (treat 10 small services as one SLO target), longer windows (compute SLO over a week instead of an hour for low-traffic surfaces), or a hybrid where the SLO is reported but alerting is event-based rather than rate-based. Google's workbook explicitly calls out this case; many production-SLO platforms (Nobl9, Pyrra, Sloth) ship low-traffic adjustments out of the box.</p>
    <p><strong>The SRE workbook's six approaches to alerting — and why five fail.</strong> Approach 1 (raw error rate over SLO) fires constantly on noise. Approach 2 (require N consecutive minutes) hides slow-burn outages. Approach 3 (longer window, e.g. 1 hour) reduces noise but delays detection and lengthens reset. Approach 4 (increment alert duration) is a hack that bolts on a slow second alert; complex and brittle. Approach 5 (single burn-rate threshold, e.g. 14.4x over 1h) is closer but has the long-reset problem. Approach 6 (MWMBR) is the only one that balances detection latency, recovery latency, and noise — and is the production default in Google, Datadog, Grafana, Splunk, Honeycomb, Sloth, Pyrra. Engineers re-deriving SLO alerting from scratch tend to re-invent Approach 1; the workbook chapter exists explicitly to prevent that retrace.</p>
    <p><strong>Burn-rate alerting math.</strong> For a SLO target S and a window of length W, the burn rate B is the multiplier by which the current error rate exceeds the budget rate. The budget rate is (1 − S) per unit time. So an actual error rate r corresponds to burn rate B = r / (1 − S). Budget consumed over window W = B × W / period (where period = SLO window, e.g. 28 days). The MWMBR canonical row "B = 14.4, W = 1h" computes: 14.4 × 1h / (28 × 24h) ≈ 2.14% of budget burnt in 1 hour. Page at this rate ensures you have time to respond before the budget is gone. The slower rows give earlier warning of slow burns: 1x × 3 days / 28 days ≈ 10.7% budget burn in 3 days, ticket-worthy but not page-worthy. These thresholds aren't arbitrary — they come from solving "what burn rate would consume X% of the budget in window W?" and picking X = 2%, 5%, 10% as the action triggers.</p>
    <p><strong>SLI selection: the iceberg failures.</strong> The SLI that breaks teams is the one that fails to correlate with user pain. Classic mistake: define an availability SLI on 5xx responses only, miss 4xx-due-to-internal-bug (e.g. a 422 thrown because of a serialization error in the service), miss high-latency-but-success (a request that took 30s but returned 200), miss correctness errors (returned 200 OK but with stale data). The senior pattern: SLI selection starts with "what is a bad outcome for the user?" then derives the measurement. For a checkout service: a bad outcome is a charge failure OR a charge that took too long OR a charge confirmed for the user but missing from the backend. Each is a different SLI ratio. Multiple SLIs per journey is normal; the SLO platform aggregates them with worst-of joins.</p>
    <p><strong>Composite SLOs and dependency math.</strong> A user journey through n independent services with SLOs S₁ … Sₙ has a journey SLO ceiling of ∏Sᵢ. With dependent failures (correlated outages: noisy neighbour, shared dependency, regional incident), the ceiling is even lower because failures cluster in time. Two patterns mitigate. First: parallel hedging — fire two requests to two replicas, take the first that succeeds — turns 99.9% × 99.9% into 1 − (0.001)² ≈ 99.9999%, dramatically better at the cost of double traffic. Second: idempotent retries with backoff — converts transient failures into successes — at the cost of latency. Both are SLO levers, but they're not free. Decisions like "should we add a retry?" become measurable: estimate the failure-rate reduction, multiply by traffic, compare to error-budget gain. The SLO turns architectural decisions into arithmetic.</p>
    <p><strong>SLA vs SLO: do not confuse them.</strong> An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is a contractual commitment to customers, typically with financial penalties for breach. An SLO (Service Level Objective) is an internal engineering target. SLAs are looser than SLOs by design — typical pattern: SLA is 99.9%, internal SLO is 99.95%. The 0.05% gap is the buffer between "engineering can fix it before customers notice" and "lawyers get involved." Mixing them is a common rookie mistake: setting SLO == SLA means every internal incident is a contract-breach risk. Set SLO tighter than SLA, alert on SLO burns, and the SLA basically takes care of itself.</p>
    <p><strong>Latency SLOs and the "happiness threshold" choice.</strong> A latency SLO needs a threshold: how slow is "slow enough to count as a failure"? For interactive UI: 100-200ms is the perceptual boundary above which users notice. For API calls: 1s is the threshold above which retries get triggered. For batch: minutes-to-hours, depending on context. The threshold sits on a bucket boundary in the histogram, otherwise the SLO is unevaluable. Multi-threshold SLOs ("90% under 100ms AND 99% under 500ms") catch tail-latency hiding behind a single percentile; they're the production-grade pattern. The Google workbook documents this as the two-threshold SLO and recommends it whenever the slow-tail behaviour matters more than the median.</p>
    <p><strong>SLO platforms and the rule-generation layer.</strong> Sloth, Pyrra, Nobl9, OpenSLO are tools that take a high-level SLO definition (YAML: SLI, target, window) and generate the underlying Prometheus recording rules, alerts, and dashboards. The benefit is consistency: every team in the org gets the same alerting shape (MWMBR with the canonical 14.4x / 6x / 1x burn rates) and the same dashboard panels, without re-deriving the math per service. The OpenSLO spec is the emerging open standard; Nobl9 founded it, and Grafana / Datadog / Honeycomb increasingly support it. Adoption is the lever: tooling consistency turns SLO-as-arithmetic into SLO-as-culture, and culture is the bottleneck on reliability investment more often than tooling.</p>
    <p><strong>Real production failures.</strong> Stripe 2022: a checkout SLO at 99.99% was internally violated (engineering noticed) but the external SLA (99.9%) was met. The team's pre-defined error budget policy auto-froze new feature deploys for 3 days while the reliability team investigated — preventing a second incident on the still-degraded code path. GitHub 2023: their SLO platform mis-counted background-job failures as user-facing for a quarter, eating their reliability budget and triggering a culture-of-blame conversation; postmortem reset the SLI definition to journey-level only. Coinbase 2024: a multi-region deploy violated their 99.99% trading API SLO for 8 minutes due to a misconfigured load balancer; the error budget policy kicked in within 24 hours, and the team paused new region launches for a week. Netflix 2024: internal SLO for video playback was loosened from 99.99% to 99.95% after a six-month review showed users couldn't perceive the difference at 99.95% but the engineering cost was significantly higher to chase the extra nine. The pattern across all four: SLO drives engineering decisions, not the other way around.</p>
    <p><strong>Observability for SLOs themselves.</strong> The meta-question: how do you know the SLO platform is working? Three signals. First: a "ratio_total" recording rule should never go to NaN — if it does, your SLI denominator is zero (low traffic edge case). Second: the burn rate over time should be stationary on average (long-window burn rate ≈ 1 means you're hitting the SLO exactly; > 1 means you'll miss; < 1 means you're over-investing). Third: the error budget policy outcomes should match the burn pattern — if you've had a high-burn quarter and no freezes happened, the policy is being ignored. Mature observability teams put SLO meta-metrics on a separate dashboard ("SLOs of SLOs"): how many active SLOs, how many are in burn, average remaining budget, time-since-last-freeze. The pattern parallels self-monitoring of Prometheus from the prior piece.</p>
    <p><strong>Security and SLOs.</strong> Two intersections. First: a successful but malicious request counts as good in the availability SLI — a credential-stuffing attack that returns 200 OK passes the SLO. Bot traffic skews the denominator and can mask real user issues; the senior pattern is to compute SLOs over filtered traffic (drop known bots, rate-limited IPs, abusive sessions) so the SLI tracks real-user health. Second: an SLO breach can itself be a security signal — an availability drop with no infra cause may be the first symptom of a DDoS or a backend exploit. Several incident-response playbooks include "check SLO burn rate" as a step in the security-incident checklist.</p>
    <p><strong>The bigger picture.</strong> An SLO is not a number; it is a contract that converts product decisions into engineering arithmetic. The error budget is the bridge between "we want to ship" and "we want to be reliable." The MWMBR alert is the bridge between "the budget is being spent" and "wake the engineer up." The error budget policy is the bridge between the alert and the org chart — without a signed policy, none of the math matters. The reason this framework outlives every monitoring tool generation is that it doesn't depend on tools: it depends on the team having committed to one number that everyone — product, engineering, operations — agrees is the truth. Tools change; the SLO survives every migration.</p>
