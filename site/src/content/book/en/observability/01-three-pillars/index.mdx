This pillar opens with a vocabulary lesson. Cindy Sridharan's 2018 O'Reilly book *Distributed Systems Observability* crystallised the term "three pillars" — logs, metrics, traces — as the three telemetry shapes a production system emits. Seven years later the phrase shows up in every job description and every vendor pitch deck, and the shape of the debate has shifted: Charity Majors and the Honeycomb crew now argue the pillars are a 1990s storage-cost artifact, not a natural taxonomy of what engineers need to know. This piece teaches the pillars as they actually work in production — what each signal is good at, what it costs, where it lies — and ends with the senior-grade view that the pillars are converging back into one shape (wide, structured events) for cost and correlation reasons. Later pieces in this pillar zoom in: structured logging (piece 02), OpenTelemetry (piece 03), the two methodology checklists RED and USE (piece 04), SLOs and error budgets (piece 05), trace context propagation (piece 06), continuous profiling (piece 07), and a capstone (piece 08).

<Crux>Logs, metrics, traces — each is the cheapest answer to a different question. The question picks the pillar, not the other way around.</Crux>

The three signals are not redundant. They differ in *what they preserve* and *what they discard*. Metrics throw away every dimension except the few you labelled — that is what makes them small and queryable across years of history, and that is what makes them blind to anything you forgot to label. Logs preserve everything you wrote in the message — every field, every string, every customer ID — and pay for it in ingestion bytes and query cost. Traces preserve the causal chain of one request across services — at the cost of sampling, since storing every span of every request would dwarf the original traffic. Each pillar exists because no single storage shape can be all three of cheap to query, high cardinality, and complete. Senior engineers do not pick "metrics-first" or "logs-first" as a religion; they pick the signal that answers the question at hand, knowing the cost model of each.

    <p><strong>One sentence.</strong> Three pillars = logs, metrics, traces — the three telemetry shapes engineers use to answer "what happened in production."</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Every incident is a search problem. Three pillars give three starting points; knowing which is cheapest for which question is the gap between 5-minute and 5-hour triage.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> A restaurant kitchen. <strong>Metrics</strong> are wall dials — covers per hour, average ticket time, error rate. Cheap to read, always-on. <strong>Logs</strong> are order tickets on the rail — every dish, every modifier, every table. Bulky, but when a customer complains you go to their ticket. <strong>Traces</strong> are the story of one specific order: prep at A, sauté at B, plate-up, runner, table. Sampled — tracing every dish would bury you — but a cold dish lights the whole path.</p>
    <p><strong>Bea and Sven.</strong> <PersonaTag id="bea" lang="en" /> gets paged: checkout error rate jumped to 3%. She opens metrics — sees the spike at 14:02 UTC. Three signals, three jobs, seconds each.</p>
    <p><strong>One scenario.</strong> An API's p99 rises from 80ms to 1.2s. Metrics say it happened, on which endpoints. Logs give error messages (or their absence — sometimes the worst slow). Traces show the exact span — DB? cache? third-party? Metrics are the alarm, logs are the diagnostic, traces are the deep dive.</p>

  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p><strong>Signal one: metrics.</strong> A metric is a numeric measurement taken at a point in time, with a small fixed set of labels (key=value pairs), aggregated into a time series. The canonical model is Prometheus: every (metric_name, label_set) combination is one time series; the series is a stream of (timestamp, value) samples typically scraped every 15s. The four standard metric types are counter (monotonically increasing, rate-of-change queryable), gauge (instantaneous value — memory used, queue depth), histogram (pre-bucketed distribution for percentile queries), and summary (per-replica percentile pre-computed). The compute model is pre-aggregation at the source: the application increments counters in-process, the scraper or push agent batches and ships them, the TSDB stores the deltas and serves queries by walking buckets. This is why metric queries return in tens of milliseconds across years of history: the heavy lifting was done at write time, not at read time.</p>
    <p><strong>Signal two: logs.</strong> A log line is an event record: a timestamp plus a payload that describes what happened. Sridharan's three forms — plaintext, structured (typically JSON), binary — capture the full range, but production observability in 2026 is structured-or-nothing: a JSON event with consistent keys (`timestamp`, `level`, `service`, `trace_id`, `message`, plus event-specific fields) is the only form that supports filter-aggregate queries at scale. The storage model is append-only ingest with indexed search: ship the event to a log pipeline (Fluent Bit, Vector, OTel Collector), buffer it, ship to a backend (Loki, ClickHouse, Elasticsearch, Datadog, Splunk), and index a subset of fields for fast filtering. Logs preserve everything you wrote — high cardinality is free at write time but expensive at query time, since the backend must scan or index every field. The cost is paid in ingest bytes (typical pricing $0.10-1.00 per GB ingested) and indexed-event count (typical pricing $1.27 per million events for Datadog's standard tier).</p>
    <p><strong>Signal three: traces.</strong> A trace is a representation of a series of causally related distributed events that encode the end-to-end request flow through a distributed system. A request entering the edge is assigned a trace_id (128-bit, globally unique); each service it touches creates a span (operation_name, start_time, duration, attributes, span_id, parent_span_id) and propagates the trace_id and current span_id forward via the W3C `traceparent` header. The collector reconstructs the tree from the spans; the UI renders a waterfall that shows where wall-clock time was spent. Storage is proportional to total span count times traffic volume — and a moderate-sized service emits 10-100 spans per request, so a 1000 req/s service produces 10k-100k spans/s, which becomes a billion spans/day uncompressed. Sampling is therefore mandatory at any non-trivial scale.</p>
    <p><strong>The cost asymmetry that produced the pillars.</strong> Why three signals and not one? Because no single storage shape is cheap on all three of: long retention, high cardinality, and full request fidelity. A time-series database (Prometheus, M3, Cortex, Mimir) is cheap to retain for years but explodes on high cardinality — a single label like `user_id` with 100k unique values multiplies series count by 100k. A logging backend is cheap on cardinality (every event is just bytes) but expensive to retain at full volume past 30 days because raw bytes accumulate fast and ingestion pricing is volumetric. A tracing backend can capture full request fidelity but only at low sampling because every retained trace is a long, fat record. The three pillars are a response to those three cost cliffs: each one optimises for the question its substrate handles cheaply, and offloads questions it cannot answer to the next pillar.</p>
    <p><strong>Cardinality is the central cost driver for metrics.</strong> Each unique combination of label values creates a separate time series in the TSDB. A metric labelled by `route` (20 templates), `method` (5 verbs), `status_class` (4 classes) has 20 &times; 5 &times; 4 = 400 series — fine. The same metric labelled by `user_id` (100k active) has 100k &times; 20 &times; 5 &times; 4 = 40M series — that is a Prometheus server out of memory at 16 GB. Grafana's published guidance and the Prometheus instrumentation guide both say the same thing: keep cardinality bounded by labelling only with values that come from a small fixed set — route templates not URLs, status classes not codes, customer segments not customer IDs. Anything high-cardinality lives in logs (free) or traces (sampled) and is joined back to the metric via exemplars (a metric sample can carry a sampled trace_id pointing at one example trace in the slow bucket).</p>
    <p><strong>Ingestion volume is the central cost driver for logs.</strong> Logs are not cardinality-constrained, but they are byte-constrained. A modest 1000 req/s service emitting 1 KB of structured log per request produces 1 MB/s = 86 GB/day = 2.6 TB/month per service. Multiply by 50 services in a typical fleet and the bill at $0.10/GB ingest plus indexed-event cost is in the tens of thousands per month before retention. The standard cost-control patterns are: log-level discipline (debug only in non-prod, info for state changes, warn for recoverable, error for actionable), sampling (1-in-100 for high-volume successful events, 100% for errors), tiered retention (15-day hot for queries, archived to cheap storage for compliance), and redirecting high-volume / low-value events out of the indexed tier — Datadog Flex Logs and Splunk's tiered indexes both formalise this.</p>
    <p><strong>Sampling is the central cost driver for traces.</strong> Two strategies dominate: head-based sampling decides at trace start whether to keep the trace (typical rate 1-10%), giving predictable cost and low overhead but a uniform random sample that under-represents rare events; tail-based sampling buffers spans until the trace completes, then decides based on full context (was there an error, did latency exceed a threshold), keeping all errors and slow tails at a much lower base rate. Tail-based is more accurate and more expensive — every span still flows through the collector even if it is eventually dropped, so collector CPU and memory scale with raw traffic, not stored volume. Production patterns: head sampling at 10% for cost, tail sampling at 1% baseline plus 100% on errors plus 100% on traces with p99-tail latency.</p>
    <p><strong>Join keys are what make the three pillars compose.</strong> Three signals on three different backends are useless if you cannot pivot between them. The bridge is a small set of shared attribute keys, formalised in OpenTelemetry's Semantic Conventions: <code>service.name</code> (which service emitted this), <code>trace_id</code> + <code>span_id</code> (which request, which step), <code>http.route</code> / <code>http.response.status_code</code> / <code>http.request.method</code> (HTTP-specific), <code>db.system</code> / <code>db.operation</code> (database calls), <code>messaging.system</code> / <code>messaging.destination.name</code> (queues). When metrics carry the same <code>service.name</code> and <code>http.route</code> as the log lines, when log lines carry the <code>trace_id</code> of the request that emitted them, when traces carry the same <code>http.route</code> attribute as the metric — that is when "click from metric spike to log line to trace" works as a smooth pivot. Without those join keys you have three disconnected dashboards and no narrative.</p>
    <p><strong>Exemplars: the bridge from metric to trace.</strong> An exemplar is a sampled trace_id attached to a histogram bucket observation. When `http_request_duration_seconds` records a 1.5s observation in the &gt;1s bucket, the histogram client optionally remembers one trace_id that produced that observation. Grafana renders these as little dots on top of the histogram heatmap; clicking the dot opens the corresponding trace. The pattern formalised in Prometheus 2.32 (early 2022) and now standard in OpenTelemetry exporters eliminates the "metric says something is slow but I have no example" gap that defined the pre-exemplar era. It is the single most important correlation primitive across the stack: aggregate metric for cheap, click-through to one concrete request for detail.</p>
    <p><strong>OpenTelemetry as the integration layer.</strong> The OpenTelemetry project (CNCF, born 2019 from a merger of OpenCensus and OpenTracing) standardises three things: an API (the surface engineers write code against), an SDK (the per-language implementation), and a wire format (OTLP — OpenTelemetry Protocol, a binary protobuf-over-gRPC-or-HTTP). The pitch: instrument once, route anywhere. Application code calls <code>tracer.start_span()</code>, the SDK builds OTLP messages, the OTel Collector forwards them to whichever backends you choose — Honeycomb, Datadog, Jaeger, ClickHouse, Loki, Prometheus, anything that speaks OTLP or has a contrib exporter. The piece 03 in this pillar covers OTel architecture in detail; here it matters because it is the substrate that makes the three pillars share join keys in practice. Without OTel Semantic Conventions, every team invents their own field names and joins fail at integration time.</p>

  <Fragment slot="senior">
    <p><strong>Observability 2.0: the pillars-as-artifact critique.</strong> Charity Majors (Honeycomb co-founder, the CTO who put the word "observability" on the engineering map in 2017) argues in her 2024-2025 blog series that the three pillars are not a natural taxonomy — they are an artifact of the storage limits of the late-2000s. Metrics existed because you couldn't afford to keep every event; logs existed because you couldn't aggregate them at query time; traces existed because you needed cross-service causality and neither of the other two carried it. With columnar storage engines, cheap object storage, and modern query planners, all three can collapse back into one shape: <em>wide structured events</em> — one log line per unit of work, carrying every field including high-cardinality ones (user_id, build_sha, feature_flag_state, region, ...), stored in a single columnar engine, queried with arbitrary slicing. Honeycomb's product is this; ClickHouse-based observability stacks (Signoz, Sentry's new backend, Cloudflare's internal stack) are this; the Honeycomb blog post "OpenTelemetry Is Not Three Pillars" explicitly recasts OTel signals as "ways to ship and store a single underlying telemetry model." The senior engineer's question is not "is 2.0 right" but "is the cost basis of 2.0 viable for my workload" — for many shops it is, for some (cheap cold metrics for years of dashboards) the 1.0 pillar split is still cost-optimal.</p>
    <p><strong>Wide events vs three signals: what changes at the engineering level.</strong> In a "1.0" stack each request emits to three places: counter increments to the metric SDK, log lines to the log SDK, spans to the trace SDK. Each signal is sampled or aggregated differently, fields are denormalised, and the join across them depends on shared join keys (trace_id, service.name) being plumbed consistently. In a "2.0" stack each request emits one wide event per service boundary it crosses — a JSON-shaped record with all attributes (user_id, route, status, duration, build_sha, feature_flags, trace_id) — and the backend computes metrics by GROUP BY at query time, retrieves logs by filtering, reconstructs traces by joining on trace_id. The columnar engine makes all three queries fast over the same data. The engineering cost: you need a backend that can scan billions of wide events per query (Honeycomb, ClickHouse, Snowflake-as-observability, Datadog Husky). The win: every field is available for every question, with no upfront cardinality budget — including the questions you didn't think to ask in advance.</p>
    <p><strong>The cost cliff is moving — and so is the pricing model.</strong> 1.0 pricing is series-month for metrics, GB-ingest plus indexed-event-count for logs, span-month for traces — three separate meters, each with its own runaway-cost mode. 2.0 pricing is closer to traffic-month plus a function of dimensionality (how rich each event is). The major cost drivers in 2.0, per Charity Majors's writings: traffic volume, architectural sprawl (how many service boundaries an event crosses), and density of instrumentation (how much you record per event). Cardinality is no longer a cost driver in 2.0, which is the most important shift — it eliminates the discipline (also the productivity tax) of constantly editing label sets to stay within budget. Trade-off: 2.0 backends are fewer and tend to be SaaS-only; 1.0 backends are commoditised across many vendors with strong open-source paths.</p>
    <p><strong>What stays the same regardless of 1.0/2.0.</strong> The semantic conventions matter either way. Whether you have three signals or one unified model, every record needs the same join keys — service.name, trace_id, http.route, db.system, customer-segment — to make slicing across teams possible. OpenTelemetry's Semantic Conventions are the de-facto standard in both worlds, and adopting them early is the highest-leverage investment in observability hygiene. The actual telemetry shapes (counters, histograms, log lines, spans) are an artifact of the backend; the field names and the discipline of populating them consistently is what makes the data useful in any stack. Senior teams that have migrated 1.0 → 2.0 or 2.0 → multi-vendor report that the migration cost is dominated by retrofitting field names, not by re-wiring the SDKs.</p>
    <p><strong>The vendor-lock-in question.</strong> 1.0 instrumentations historically coupled application code to a specific vendor SDK (Datadog's `dd-trace`, New Relic's agent). Switching vendors meant rewriting instrumentation. The OpenTelemetry bet is that a single open API (the OTel SDK) plus a single open wire format (OTLP) plus a routable collector means you can swap backends without touching application code. In practice, OTel APIs cover traces and metrics well, logs less well (the OTel Logs API stabilised in late 2023; SDKs in late 2024); semantic conventions are still maturing per domain (HTTP and database are GA, messaging and FaaS are partial). The honest senior take: OTel is the right bet for new instrumentation in 2026 if portability matters; if you are already deep in a vendor SDK and the vendor's product is what you want, the migration cost may not pay back. Either way, OTel SDKs can coexist with vendor SDKs during transition.</p>
    <p><strong>Cardinality budgets as an engineering practice.</strong> In any 1.0 metrics stack, run a cardinality budget per service per metric. Mature shops set a per-team limit (e.g. 100k active series total across all services on the team's namespace) and enforce it with CI checks that flag new labels above the threshold. Tools: Grafana's mimirtool, Datadog's Custom Metrics Usage view, Prometheus's `prometheus_tsdb_head_series` and `count by (__name__) ({__name__=~".+"})`. A real production pattern (Cloudflare 2022 post-mortem): every team owns its cardinality budget and pays for overages in lost capacity at the central Prometheus tier; the budget is reviewed quarterly and resized based on actual usage. The cultural payoff is that engineers think about labels at write time, not after the OOM at 03:00.</p>
    <p><strong>PII risk on the metric and log surfaces.</strong> Both metrics labels and log fields can leak PII into a less-audited datastore than the source database. Real incidents: a payments company in 2021 leaked customer phone numbers as a metric label (`failed_phone="+1..."`); a SaaS company in 2023 ingested raw request bodies into logs (including session tokens) for two months before a customer noticed in a support thread; a marketplace in 2024 emitted user search queries as a `query` span attribute, exposing demographic-segment information to the BI team. Mitigations: an allow-list of permitted metric labels and span attributes (deny by default), redaction in the logging pipeline (Vector and Fluent Bit both ship with regex redactors for common PII patterns), and audit dashboards that show high-cardinality string fields per signal. Treat label review and field review as a security gate, not a perf gate.</p>
    <p><strong>The four-pillar reality: profiles join the stack.</strong> Continuous profiling — Pyroscope, Parca, Datadog Profiler, Polar Signals — adds a fourth signal: sampled stack-trace data showing CPU time per function over time. Profiles answer questions that traces cannot (where inside this function does the time go?) and that metrics cannot (which line of code is hot today vs yesterday?). The semantic-conventions and join-key story is the same: profiles emitted with `service.name`, `trace_id` on the hot stack, and the `code.function` attribute pivot cleanly to traces and logs. The piece 07 in this pillar covers profiling in depth; here it matters that "three pillars" is already an undercount in any 2026 production setup. Honeycomb and others now talk about "signal types" rather than "pillars" precisely to avoid lock-in to the number three.</p>
    <p><strong>Real production failure modes per signal.</strong> Metrics: cardinality bombs (a single careless label takes down the TSDB; Cloudflare 2022 lost regional Prometheus for 90 minutes). Logs: ingestion runaway (a single chatty service quadruples the bill; the Datadog Logs out-of-band incident in many companies); index-tier overflow (queries time out because too much is indexed); silent PII leaks. Traces: sampling gaps (the interesting trace was the one not sampled; mitigation is tail-based + 100% on errors); span explosion (a poorly-instrumented loop emits 10k spans per request, blowing memory in the collector); orphaned spans (context propagation broken across an async boundary, traces split into multiple fragments). Profiles: overhead misjudged in production (set the sample rate too high and the profiler eats 5% of CPU); symbol-resolution misses (stripped binaries make profiles unreadable). Each failure mode has a detection metric — the meta-monitoring of the monitoring tier, again — and senior teams build dashboards for these as carefully as for their product services.</p>
    <p><strong>OpenTelemetry sampling configuration in practice.</strong> The OTel Sampler API offers head-based decisions (AlwaysOn, AlwaysOff, TraceIdRatioBased, ParentBased) at the SDK and tail-based at the collector (TailSamplingProcessor). A common production layout: SDK is ParentBased with a 100% root sampler for cheap workloads or a TraceIdRatioBased 10-20% root sampler for heavy workloads, ensuring downstream services follow the parent's decision via the W3C `traceparent` `sampled` flag. The collector then layers tail sampling on top with policies like `status_code = ERROR`, `latency &gt; 1s`, `service.name = critical-service`. The Elastic Observability Labs post-mortem on head-based sampling (2024) is required reading on why naive head sampling under-represents errors and slow tails — the trace_id used to compute the random decision must be uncorrelated with request properties, which is harder than it looks if the trace_id is generated from the request path.</p>
    <p><strong>The 1990s-and-2010s cultural debt the pillars inherit.</strong> A surprising amount of pillar-shaped tooling in 2026 is shaped by decisions made when storage was the dominant cost: graphite was designed for 1990s-disk RPMs; ELK was designed for 2010s SSDs; Prometheus was designed for in-RAM TSDBs on commodity boxes (2012 GCE was the limit, single-server). The decisions made sense then; they shape what is easy and what is expensive now. Senior engineers can spot the seams: averaging vs histograms (averages were cheap on 90s disks), pre-aggregation vs raw event retention (raw was infeasible until S3 hit $0.023/GB), structured logs vs grep-friendly text (text was the lowest common denominator before JSON became default in clients). The shift to 2.0 is best understood not as a generational fight but as an engineering re-evaluation in light of newer cost basis. The pillars are not wrong; they are <em>pre-2020 cost-optimal</em>.</p>

<KeyTakeaway>Three pillars = three cost shapes: metrics aggregate cheaply but discard dimensions, logs preserve dimensions but pay in bytes, traces preserve causality but must sample. Pick the signal that answers the question cheapest.</KeyTakeaway>

<SpiralCue thread="latency">
Each pillar trades a different shape of latency. Metrics return in milliseconds because they are pre-aggregated; logs scan or index at read time, paying per-query latency that scales with volume; traces are fast per trace but require sampling, which is its own latency-vs-cost tradeoff. Every piece in this pillar revisits one of those shapes — structured logging (piece 02), OpenTelemetry's wire format (piece 03), trace propagation overhead (piece 06), continuous profiling cost (piece 07).
