A queue connects a producer that does not know who will read its messages to a consumer that does not know who wrote them. Decoupling is the win. The cost is that the two sides now have to agree on what "delivered" means when the network drops packets, the broker crashes, the consumer dies mid-handler. There are three answers — at-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once — and only two of them are actually achievable. Knowing which is which keeps a payment system from charging a card twice or losing a transfer to the void.

<Crux>Exactly-once delivery is impossible. Exactly-once processing ships. Idempotent producer + consumer dedup gets you there.</Crux>

The mental model: every message-passing system has three places a failure can hide. The producer-to-broker leg (did the broker actually receive what I sent?), the broker's own durability (is the message persisted before I crash?), the broker-to-consumer leg (did the consumer actually process before it crashed?). At each leg the network can lose, duplicate, or reorder. Delivery semantics name the contract that survives those failures. At-most-once accepts loss; at-least-once accepts duplicates; exactly-once eliminates both — but only by adding coordination that makes the protocol distributed-transaction-shaped. The Two Generals' Problem proves you cannot have pure exactly-once delivery over an unreliable channel; you can only fake it by making the consumer's handler idempotent so duplicates are harmless.

    <p><strong>What delivery guarantees are in one sentence.</strong> The contract between producer, broker, and consumer about how many times a message may be processed when something fails — zero (at-most-once), at least one (at-least-once), or exactly one (exactly-once).</p>
    <p><strong>Why care.</strong> Without a clear contract, the same payment may be charged twice on a retry, the same email sent five times to angry customers, or a critical event silently lost. Picking the wrong guarantee is the difference between a glitch and a lawsuit.</p>
    <p><strong>The metaphor.</strong> A courier delivers letters. <em>At-most-once</em>: drops each once; if recipient was out, lost — but never two copies. <em>At-least-once</em>: knocks until signed for; reply lost? knocks again — possible double-sign. <em>Exactly-once</em>: tracking number on each; recipient checks log; duplicates shredded.</p>

  <Fragment slot="middle">
    <p><strong>The three legs of failure.</strong> Every queue lives between three communications and any one of them can fail in three ways. Producer → broker: the network drops the send, drops the broker's ack, or the broker crashes after persisting but before acking. Broker durability: the broker acks the producer but loses the message before delivering to a consumer (replication lag, fsync skipped, disk failure). Broker → consumer: the broker delivers, the consumer processes, but crashes before acking — or acks before processing. Each of the three legs has a "fast" mode (no ack expected, message considered delivered) and a "safe" mode (ack required, retry on failure). Picking different combinations gives you the three delivery semantics.</p>
    <p><strong>At-most-once: fire and forget.</strong> The producer sends and does not wait for ack; the broker stores and does not require a consumer ack to discard; the consumer processes and does not retry on failure. If anything fails, the message is lost. The benefit is minimum latency and zero coordination — a UDP-style send on top of any transport. Use case: metrics, debug logs, fire-and-forget telemetry where one lost sample out of a million does not matter. Anti-use: anything where a missing message has business consequences. The discipline is that the application must not assume any message was delivered just because send() returned successfully.</p>
    <p><strong>At-least-once: ack and retry.</strong> The producer sends and waits for the broker's ack — on timeout or error, it retries. The broker stores durably (persisted to disk, replicated to N-1 followers) before acking. The consumer reads the message, processes it, and then explicitly acks the broker. If the consumer does not ack within a visibility timeout (SQS) or rejoin commit deadline (Kafka), the broker redelivers. This catches every kind of failure but at the cost of duplicates: any time an ack is lost, the message is processed twice (or more). At-least-once is the default of SQS, RabbitMQ's "with ack", and Kafka with default settings (acks=all, retries enabled).</p>
    <p><strong>Exactly-once is two protocols, not one.</strong> "Exactly-once delivery" means the broker delivers each message to the consumer exactly once even under failure. "Exactly-once processing" means the consumer's side effect (write to DB, charge a card, send an email) happens exactly once even when delivery duplicates. The first is impossible over an unreliable channel — the Two Generals' Problem proves no number of acks can guarantee both sides know the same fact. The second is achievable in two ways: (a) consumer-side idempotency (consumer keeps a dedup log keyed by message ID, skips duplicates), or (b) transactional consumer that atomically processes and acks in one DB transaction — if the transaction commits, the message is acked; if it aborts, the broker redelivers.</p>
    <p><strong>Kafka's idempotent producer (KIP-98).</strong> The producer is assigned a unique producer-ID at startup and each message carries a monotonically increasing per-partition sequence number. The broker tracks the latest sequence per producer-ID-and-partition; duplicate sequences (same producer ID, same partition, sequence already seen) are silently deduplicated. Out-of-order sequences are rejected. This eliminates the duplicates caused by producer retries — the broker sees the retried message as "I already have sequence 5 from producer X" and acks without writing. Enabled via <code>enable.idempotence=true</code>; cost is ~3% throughput. Available since Kafka 0.11 (mid-2017).</p>
    <p><strong>Kafka transactions: atomic multi-partition write + offset commit.</strong> The transactional producer wraps a batch of writes-to-multiple-partitions plus a consumer-offset-commit in one transaction managed by a transaction coordinator broker. Either all writes plus the offset commit become visible to downstream consumers, or none do — atomic across the cluster. This is the basis of stream-processing exactly-once: a Kafka Streams job reads from input topic, writes to output topic, commits the input offset all in one transaction. If the job crashes mid-flight, the transaction aborts; on restart, the input message is redelivered, the partial output is rolled back. Configured via <code>transactional.id</code> and <code>isolation.level=read_committed</code> on the downstream consumer; cost is 20-30% throughput overhead due to the two-phase-commit protocol.</p>
    <p><strong>Consumer-side dedup: the cheap effectively-once.</strong> When transactional brokers are unavailable or undesirable (operational complexity, multi-broker pipelines), the canonical pattern is at-least-once delivery with consumer-side dedup. Producer assigns each message a unique ID (UUID, snowflake, etc). Consumer maintains a dedup store keyed by message ID. The dedup check runs in the same transaction as the side effect: in SQL, <code>INSERT INTO dedup (id) VALUES ('msg-7a3f')</code> then <code>do the work</code> then <code>COMMIT</code> — the INSERT fails with unique-constraint violation on duplicate, rolling back the entire transaction including the work. The dedup row is the receipt that says "I have already processed this." Dedup table grows; sweep entries older than the maximum redelivery window (typically a few hours).</p>
    <p><strong>SQS visibility timeout in detail.</strong> SQS does not have an ack-or-fail RPC; it has visibility timeout. When a consumer receives a message, the broker hides the message from other consumers for the timeout duration (default 30s, max 12 hours). If the consumer deletes the message within that window, it is gone. If the timeout fires before deletion, the message becomes visible to any consumer again — at-least-once delivery. The pitfalls: visibility timeout too short means slow-processing consumers see their message redelivered while they are still working on it (concurrent duplicate processing); visibility timeout too long means a crashed consumer holds the message hidden for hours, slowing recovery. Rule of thumb: visibility timeout = 6x average processing time, with extension hooks (<code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code>) for slow batches.</p>
    <p><strong>Dead-letter queues and the redrive policy.</strong> When a consumer fails to process a message N times (configured as <code>maxReceiveCount</code>, recommended ≥5), the broker moves it to a dead-letter queue (DLQ). The DLQ holds poison-pill messages — usually a payload that triggers a bug in the consumer. Without a DLQ, the same message blocks the queue forever, redelivered endlessly. The DLQ lets the system make progress on other messages while a human (or scheduled re-drive job) inspects the poison. AWS SQS supports a redrive policy to send DLQ messages back to the source queue after the bug is fixed. Operational discipline: alert on DLQ depth, never let it grow unbounded, and investigate top message types.</p>
    <p><strong>The outbox pattern: producer-side reliability.</strong> The producer side of the dual-write problem — application updates its database and publishes a message — has its own at-least-once failure shape. If the producer's send to the broker fails after the DB commit, the broker never sees the event. The outbox pattern fixes this: in the same DB transaction as the business update, INSERT a row into an "outbox" table. A separate sender process reads outbox rows and publishes them. If the application crashes after DB commit but before publish, the outbox row is still there for the next sender run. CDC-based outbox (Debezium reads the DB transaction log) is the modern shape because it does not require a poll loop. The outbox is the producer-side equivalent of consumer-side dedup: both treat the broker as at-least-once and put correctness in a transactional store on either side.</p>
    <p><strong>FIFO vs standard: ordering tradeoff.</strong> SQS FIFO and Kafka per-partition both guarantee ordering, at significant throughput cost. SQS FIFO is capped at ~300 transactions per second per message-group; standard SQS has effectively unlimited throughput but no ordering guarantee. Kafka achieves ordering within a partition by serialising all reads/writes for that partition through one broker; cross-partition order is undefined. The design implication: pick the smallest possible partitioning key (per-user, per-tenant, per-resource). A user-events stream partitioned by user-id has order within a user but not across — usually exactly what the application wants. Partitioning by something coarse (per-region) means low parallelism and contention; partitioning by something fine (per-event) means no ordering and full parallelism. The choice constrains throughput, latency, and concurrent consumer count.</p>
    <p><strong>Consumer groups and rebalancing.</strong> Kafka and SQS scale consumers horizontally by adding workers. Kafka uses consumer groups: each partition is assigned to one consumer in the group, partition count caps maximum parallelism. SQS lets any number of consumers poll the queue; FIFO routes by message-group, standard by random. When a consumer joins or leaves (deploy, crash, autoscale), the broker rebalances: paused processing, partitions reassigned, processing resumed. Rebalances can take seconds to tens of seconds during which no messages are processed — and any in-flight messages from the leaving consumer become visible again for the new owner. Mature pipelines minimise rebalance frequency (sticky assignment in Kafka 2.4+, cooperative-sticky in 2.5+) and idempotent consumers so the redelivery during rebalance is harmless.</p>

    <p><strong>Two Generals' Problem: the impossibility proof in detail.</strong> Two generals want to coordinate an attack at dawn. They can only communicate via messenger across enemy territory; messengers may be captured. Can they agree to attack? Proof by contradiction: suppose a protocol exists that lets them agree. Consider the last message in the protocol that always succeeds. If we remove it, the protocol still works (because the sender already had certainty when sending the last message, and the recipient is irrelevant by definition of "last"). So the protocol has the same property with one fewer message; by induction, the protocol works with zero messages — clearly absurd, since they cannot coordinate without communicating. The result generalises: no finite protocol over an unreliable channel can give both parties certainty of mutual agreement. Exactly-once delivery requires both parties to know the message was delivered exactly once; impossible by Two Generals. Exactly-once processing only requires one party (the consumer) to know; achievable with consumer-side dedup. This asymmetry is why production systems can implement "effectively-once" but not "exactly-once-delivery".</p>
    <p><strong>Kafka exactly-once: the full protocol.</strong> Three pieces in concert. (1) Idempotent producer: <code>enable.idempotence=true</code> assigns a producer-ID and per-partition sequence; broker dedups retries. (2) Transactional producer: <code>transactional.id=app-v1</code> binds the producer to a transaction coordinator. Calls to <code>beginTransaction</code>, <code>send</code> (across multiple partitions), <code>sendOffsetsToTransaction</code> (with input consumer's offsets), <code>commitTransaction</code> — all atomic from the consumer's perspective. (3) Consumer reads with <code>isolation.level=read_committed</code> so uncommitted (aborted) transaction messages are filtered out. The coordinator broker writes a special transaction-marker record into every involved partition's log; consumers skip records belonging to aborted transactions. The two-phase commit happens between the coordinator and the partition leaders. KIP-98 documents the protocol in full. Shipped in Kafka 0.11.0 (mid-2017), refined in subsequent releases (KIP-447 reduced overhead in Kafka Streams, KIP-936 added stronger fencing for zombie producers in 2024).</p>
    <p><strong>Hybrid exactly-once: Kafka + transactional database.</strong> Real production rarely fits inside one Kafka cluster — typically a stream job reads Kafka and writes to a DB. Pure Kafka exactly-once does not extend across this boundary; the gap between Kafka commit and DB commit is where duplicates leak. Two patterns. <em>Read-process-write within Kafka</em>: input topic, transformation, output topic, all-Kafka, exactly-once via transactions. Used by Kafka Streams. <em>Read-process-write-to-DB with idempotent DB writes</em>: at-least-once Kafka delivery, consumer wraps DB write in a per-offset idempotent transaction. The DB primary key includes a stable identifier (Kafka offset, message UUID, business key). Each retry hits ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING. Most production stream-to-DB pipelines use this; it is simpler and faster than chasing distributed transactions across Kafka and Postgres.</p>
    <p><strong>SQS visibility timeout: the per-message contract.</strong> When SQS delivers a message, it records a per-message timestamp and the consumer's receipt handle. Until the timestamp + visibility timeout passes, no other consumer sees the message. The consumer extends the timeout via <code>ChangeMessageVisibility(receiptHandle, timeoutSeconds)</code> if processing runs long. The exact mechanics: SQS's internal counter per-message is checked on every ReceiveMessage call; messages with current_time &gt; receive_time + visibility_timeout are eligible for delivery. On consumer DeleteMessage with the matching receiptHandle, the message is removed. Pitfall: visibility timeout resets to default on redelivery (not the value you set via ChangeMessageVisibility — that was scoped to the previous receipt). If your consumer's normal path extends to 90 seconds but the default queue visibility is 30, every redelivered message starts with 30 seconds and may be redelivered again before the slow consumer finishes. Set queue default visibility to match the slowest expected processing.</p>
    <p><strong>Visibility extension as long-polling: the heartbeat pattern.</strong> For consumers with variable or long processing times, the production pattern is a heartbeat: every N seconds while processing, call <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> to bump the timeout. If the consumer crashes, heartbeats stop, the timeout expires naturally, the broker redelivers. This decouples the timeout from worst-case processing time. AWS SDKs implement this in some clients (Java's AmazonSQSExtendedClient, Boto3 helpers); custom consumers must implement the heartbeat thread themselves. Tuning: heartbeat interval = visibility_timeout / 3, so a missed heartbeat does not immediately trigger redelivery. The pattern also detects stuck consumers — if heartbeats stop because the consumer is deadlocked rather than crashed, the broker still redelivers and a different worker can pick up.</p>
    <p><strong>Stripe Idempotency-Key: the canonical external-API idempotency.</strong> Stripe accepts an <code>Idempotency-Key</code> HTTP header (typically a UUID) on POST endpoints. Stripe stores the key + first response for 24 hours. Subsequent requests with the same key return the cached response without re-executing the side effect. This pushes dedup responsibility to the external API, which is correct: only Stripe can guarantee its own internal exactly-once. Pattern: when consuming from a queue with at-least-once delivery, derive the Idempotency-Key from the message ID (or a stable hash of the business action). Reprocessing the same message reuses the key; Stripe returns the same response without charging the card again. The same pattern exists in PayPal (Request-Id), Square (idempotency_key), and most payment APIs. SES uses MessageDeduplicationId on its FIFO version; Twilio uses request-deduplication on the messaging API. For your own internal APIs, ship the same convention.</p>
    <p><strong>Poison-pill detection and quarantine.</strong> A poison pill is a message that always crashes the consumer — typically due to a bug, malformed payload, or unhandled edge case. Without DLQ, the broker redelivers the poison forever, blocking the queue (in FIFO queues, all later messages stuck behind it). DLQ + maxReceiveCount handles this: after N delivery attempts, the message moves to DLQ and the queue makes progress on other messages. Pitfalls in production: maxReceiveCount=1 means transient errors (a flaky downstream) send messages to DLQ immediately — set to 3-10 depending on tolerance. maxReceiveCount too high means the consumer thrashes on the poison for a long time before quarantine. The DLQ retention (default 14 days max for SQS) is when the poisons must be triaged — past 14 days they are lost. Production discipline: alert on DLQ depth, dashboard with per-message-type counts, weekly redrive review.</p>
    <p><strong>Ordering vs delivery semantics: orthogonal concerns.</strong> A common confusion: "I need exactly-once and FIFO" is two independent requirements. Kafka guarantees order within a partition (not across partitions); SQS FIFO queues guarantee order within a message group ID. Order is a per-partition or per-group property. Delivery semantics are message-by-message. You can have at-least-once + FIFO (SQS FIFO with content-based dedup), at-least-once + unordered (standard SQS), exactly-once + FIFO (Kafka with single partition + transactions), exactly-once + unordered (Kafka multi-partition + transactions). Choosing FIFO costs throughput (lower concurrency, often 300 TPS per SQS FIFO group); choosing exactly-once costs 20-30% overhead. Pick by business need, not by reflex — most workloads do not need both.</p>
    <p><strong>Outbox pattern: producer-side exactly-once.</strong> Even sending a message to the broker is at-least-once: the producer may retry on timeout. To make producer-to-broker exactly-once at the application level, the outbox pattern: in the same DB transaction as the business update, INSERT a row into an "outbox" table. A separate sender reads the outbox, publishes to the broker, marks the row as sent. If the business commits but the sender crashes, the outbox row is still there for the next sender. If the sender crashes after publish but before mark-as-sent, the broker gets a duplicate — but the broker's idempotent producer dedups. The outbox pattern is the standard fix for "I updated the DB but the message did not get to the queue" — a real production failure shape called the dual-write problem (this chapter relates to backend's idempotency-retries piece).</p>
    <p><strong>Real production failures.</strong> Stripe 2020: a bug in their internal queue consumer skipped the Idempotency-Key on retry, charging a small percentage of customers twice during a Q4 traffic spike. Refunded within 24 hours; the post-mortem published a hardened pattern. AWS SQS 2019: a region had a visibility-timeout race where consumers extending visibility while DeleteMessage was in flight occasionally caused redelivery — fixed in next SDK release. Stripe internal 2023: a hot DLQ accumulated 1M messages during a downstream incident; the operator who tried to "redrive all" without rate-limiting overloaded the source queue and triggered cascading throttling. Slack 2022: a Kafka exactly-once stream pipeline had a transaction-coordinator bug after a broker rolling restart; the system delivered duplicates for 6 minutes during the window. The pattern: exactly-once is harder than it looks; production fault tolerance lives in the consumer's idempotency more than in the broker's guarantee.</p>
    <p><strong>Observability for delivery semantics.</strong> Per-broker metrics: <code>messages_published_total</code> by topic + producer, <code>messages_delivered_total</code> by topic + consumer-group, <code>consumer_lag_messages</code> (publisher offset minus committed offset), <code>visibility_timeout_extensions_total</code> (SQS), <code>dlq_depth</code> by source queue, <code>dlq_age_seconds_p99</code> (oldest message in DLQ — alarming when >1h). Per-consumer metrics: <code>dedup_check_hit_rate</code> (how often dedup catches a duplicate — should be near zero steady-state, spikes during failover), <code>side_effect_duration_p99</code>, <code>retry_count_p99</code>. Alerts: DLQ growth, consumer lag growth past N, dedup hit rate > 5% sustained (indicates frequent redelivery). Distributed tracing: every message carries a trace ID propagated from producer through consumer through any downstream services, so a duplicate processing event is traceable end-to-end.</p>
